**[00:53]** Yesterday we discussed classical ML models (linear models, regression tree boosting, Gaussian processes, probabilistic models with variational clustering, etc.) all following the same structure: high-dimensional input (images, sound, video) → manual feature extraction → vector of features → ML model giving output $Y$ from $X$. The deep learning revolution of the late 2000s/early 2010s switched this to end-to-end learning of a single neural network. Inside the network, the early part still performs feature extraction, producing features that are most meaningful for the desired output by virtue of training, and the final layer is typically a linear model—linear regression for a continuous output, or logistic regression for classification (which is the usual case; pure numeric outputs are rare).

**[03:58]** We then began discussing the structure of neural networks, specifically individual perceptrons (artificial neurons). A single unit is a linear combination of the inputs followed by a non-linearity. The whole point of neural networks is to build a computational graph out of such elementary functions. Composing many simple things can yield very complicated results; the question is how to tweak the composition so that the result is what we want—this is the problem of training deep networks, i.e., learning their weights. Theoretically, as long as you have any non-linearity, you can approximate anything you want.

**[06:30]** Our first brief topic is the actual non-linearities used. Originally, in the 1943 McCulloch–Pitts paper and Rosenblatt’s 1958 perceptron, people used threshold (step) functions: output 1 if $w \cdot x > A$, else 0. This works for a single perceptron, but you cannot build networks of such units. Networks of linear perceptrons are pointless because a composition of linear functions is linear, so the whole network would be equivalent to a single perceptron. With step-function units, universal approximation theorems exist, but you cannot actually find the approximation using gradient descent, our only practical training method. A piecewise constant activation has zero derivative almost everywhere, so moving weights locally gives no gradient signal.

**[09:33]** Seeking a soft approximation to the threshold idea, the field used sigmoid activation functions throughout the classical era up to around 2011–2012. A sigmoid has horizontal asymptotes on both sides and increases monotonically. The prototypical example is the logistic sigmoid: $\sigma(a) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}$, which goes from 0 to 1 and is symmetric. For internal layers, it is better to avoid saturation near zero, so a sigmoid from −1 to 1, symmetric around zero, became more common: the hyperbolic tangent, $\tanh(a)$, which goes to 1 at $+\infty$ and −1 at $-\infty$. These sigmoids were standard until about 2011–2012.

**[12:57]** Around 2011–2012, an influential paper introduced a completely different, very simple activation that worked extremely well: the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU). The ReLU function is $\text{ReLU}(a) = \max(0, a)$; it is zero for negative inputs and equal to the input for positive inputs. In contrast to piecewise constant activations, ReLU can propagate gradients on the positive side. However, if a neuron with ReLU activation receives a negative input ($w \cdot x < 0$), the gradient is zero and no learning occurs on that sample.

**[15:19]** For ReLU, when the input is negative, the output is strictly zero, so gradients stop at that point. In many architectures, especially convolutional ones, this sparsity is acceptable and even desirable. In convolutional networks the same weights are reused across the input, and it is fine when half of the activations are zeroed out.

**[16:14]** Unbounded activations like ReLU have an important advantage. Sigmoids suffer from saturation: their derivatives go to zero on both the left and right tails. ReLU goes to zero on the negative side immediately, but on the positive side it does not saturate. A sigmoid cannot distinguish between moderate, strong, and very strong activation—once the neuron is very sure, gradients vanish and learning stops. ReLU explicitly stops on the negative side, but on the positive side it never stops, allowing continued learning.

**[17:31]** There is even a mathematical result: if you stack an infinite series of sigmoids, the result approximates a function that is zero on the left and eventually becomes a straight line $y = x$ on the right. It does not have the exact kink at zero, but it soon asymptotes to that linear behavior. So a ReLU activation is like stacking many sigmoids and counting how many of them actually activate, which carries more information.

**[18:38]** Incidentally, biological neurons show a similar activation pattern. The firing rate as a function of excitation resembles a ReLU curve: first completely inactive, then a complicated phase transition, and after that a roughly linear response—which is unexpected but interesting.

**[19:45]** Biological neurons do not go to infinity; they saturate in reality. But in a computer, saturation is not necessary—we can stretch the linear region as far as we want.

**[20:30]** What ultimately mattered is that ReLU worked very well. Although the idea existed since the 1970s, when people tested it with real convolutional networks around 2011–2012, almost everyone switched to ReLUs.

**[20:59]** Research on activation functions continued. The first straightforward modification was the leaky ReLU. It is similar, but on the negative side it also has a straight line with a smaller slope. It is still a single hinge at zero, but it is non‑zero on the left, which prevents complete sparsity and preserves gradient flow.

**[22:26]** Around 2017, an automated search was performed for the best possible activation function. An activation function expressed as a formula is a computational graph composed of elementary operations. For instance, ReLU is $\max(x,0)$, logistic sigmoid is $1/(1+e^{-x})$, and hyperbolic tangent is $(e^x - e^{-x})/(e^x + e^{-x})$. Because one cannot take gradients over the graph structure, the search used an evolutionary algorithm: mutations and crossovers of graph parts, evaluating performance on a set of architectures and datasets, and keeping the best populations.

**[26:15]** This search, conducted by Google with massive compute, produced the Swish activation function. It looks like a leaky ReLU on the right, but on the left it first dips negative and then returns to zero, making it non‑monotonic. Empirically, it proved to work better, although the exact reason is not fully understood.

**[27:05]** Standard architectures, especially in computer vision, often use Swish activations. Research on activation functions continues. The standard set includes threshold, logistic sigmoid, hyperbolic tangent, and ReLU. Beyond these, there are leaky ReLUs, exponential linear units (ELU), Swish, Mish, and the Gaussian Linear Unit (GELU, also called GLU). GELU multiplies the input by the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian; it looks similar to Swish but is slightly different. It often works better in modern architectures, and current LLMs mostly use GELU. A practical sequence to try is ReLU, then Swish, then perhaps GELU, and maybe a leaky ReLU. That is where the field stands. Every subfield runs deep: wherever you look, there is a long line of work; each idea comes with confirming experiments and sometimes theoretical justification, though some survive while others fade.

**[30:44]** Now we turn to backpropagation. A neural network is a huge function that computes the loss for a given input. It has weights $w$, an input $x$ (e.g., an image), and the correct label $y$. The network outputs a prediction $\hat{y}$, and the loss $l(x, y, w)$ is computed from the prediction and the ground truth. In a binary cat–dog problem, $x$ is the image, $y = 1$ for cat and $0$ for dog. The network produces the estimated probability $\hat{y}$ for the cat class. The per-sample loss is $-\log \hat{y}$, which we minimize to push $\hat{y}$ toward $1$ for a cat. The minimization is with respect to the weights $w$ (the network contains weights, the loss function itself typically does not). In machine learning we have many data points, so the total loss $L(w)$ is the sum or average of the per-sample losses over the whole dataset. Training therefore minimizes this average loss with respect to $w$.

**[35:36]** This loss function $L(w)$ is extremely complex: it is a sum of many complicated terms, the weight vector is high-dimensional, and $L$ is hopelessly non-convex. We cannot enumerate all possible $w$ or resort to elaborate optimization procedures. Practically, the only viable approach is gradient descent; even slightly more complicated methods tend to fail. The update rule is $w_{k+1} = w_k - \eta \nabla L(w_k)$, where $w_k$ is the current weight checkpoint and we move in the direction of steepest descent. This simple idea remains the foundation of modern training algorithms.

**[38:45]** To implement gradient descent, we need to compute $\nabla L(w)$ for large, composite functions. In deep learning, a complicated function is defined as a computational graph – a directed acyclic graph where inputs and weights flow through nodes. Each node performs a simple operation (addition, multiplication, an activation, etc.) for which we can easily compute local values and derivatives. The loss $L$ emerges as the final output of this graph. The overall function is a composition of simple pieces, so we can compute derivatives locally and propagate them.

**[40:33]** A single node in a computational graph is an elementary function, such as a dot product between two vectors, or an activation function of one variable—simple functions whose derivatives and values we know. We assume the complex function is computed through a graph of such simple nodes, and we want to compute the gradient with respect to some of the inputs (including weights) at a specific point. Backpropagation is a method to do that.

**[41:40]** I'll explain with an example. Take a function of two variables, say $f(x,y) = (x+y)^2 (x-y) + y^3$. We can represent it as a computational graph where the elementary operations are $+$, $-$, square, cube, multiplication. The graph is:

- $a = x+y$
- $b = x-y$
- $c = y^3$
- $d = a^2$
- $e = d \cdot b$
- $f = e + c$

Every node is very simple; we can easily compute its value and derivative.

**[44:56]** We can use the graph to compute the value of $f$ at a point. For $x=2$, $y=-1$, forward propagation (bottom-up from inputs to output) gives: $a = 1$, $b = 3$, $c = -1$, $d = 1$, $e = 3$, $f = 2$. In one pass we get the function value.

**[46:49]** Interestingly, we can also compute derivatives with forward propagation. To find $\frac{df}{dx}$ at the same point, we start with $\frac{dx}{dx}=1$, $\frac{dy}{dx}=0$. Then, using the chain rule:

- $\frac{da}{dx}=1$, $\frac{db}{dx}=1$, $\frac{dc}{dx}=0$ (since $\frac{dy}{dx}=0$)
- $\frac{dd}{dx}=2a\cdot\frac{da}{dx}=2\cdot1=2$
- $\frac{de}{dx}=\frac{dd}{dx}\cdot b+\frac{db}{dx}\cdot d=2\cdot3+1\cdot1=7$
- $\frac{df}{dx}=\frac{de}{dx}+\frac{dc}{dx}=7+0=7$.

The symbolic formula obtained would be $\frac{df}{dx} = (x+y)^2 + 2(x+y)(x-y)$, which matches the numerical result. Notice that inside each node we only use the values already available (e.g., $a$, $b$, $d$) without needing the original $x$ and $y$.

**[52:22]** So by a single bottom-up pass we compute both the function value and the derivative with respect to one variable. Here is a question: why is that not enough?

**[53:13]** While the forward-propagation derivative we just computed is formally correct, it does not scale to the full gradient of a real neural network. If we need the gradient with respect to every input variable, we either have to traverse the computation graph once per variable – a separate pass for each of millions of weights – or carry all partial derivatives simultaneously in a single pass, which would require memory proportional to the number of parameters times the graph size. Neither approach is practical: the time or the memory would explode.

**[57:04]** To see the scaling problem, consider that for a network with a million weights, a forward-mode algorithm that computes $df/dx$ would need a million separate traversals to get the whole gradient, or a single pass that stores a vector of size one million at every node. Modern networks barely fit one copy on a GPU, so storing a million copies is impossible, and a million passes would take forever.

**[1:01:58]** Backpropagation flips the direction. Instead of starting from the inputs and computing $d(\text{everything})/dx$, we start from the output and compute $df/d(\text{everything})$. The induction base is $df/df = 1$. Going top‑down: $df/de = 1$, $df/dc = 1$. For $d$, which only affects $f$ through $e$, the chain rule gives $df/dd = (df/de)(de/dd) = 1 \cdot b = b$. Similarly, $df/db = (df/de)(de/db) = 1 \cdot d = d$. For $a$, $df/da = (df/dd)(dd/da) = b \cdot 2a = 2ab$. For an input like $x$ that feeds multiple downstream nodes, we sum the contributions: $df/dx = (df/da)(da/dx) + (df/db)(db/dx) = 2ab \cdot 1 + d \cdot 1 = 2ab + d$, which expands to $2(x+y)(x-y) + (x+y)^2$ – the same result as before. Without re‑running the graph, we can immediately compute $df/dy$ by summing over all paths: $df/dy = (df/da)(da/dy) + (df/db)(db/dy) + (df/dc)(dc/dy) = 2ab \cdot 1 + d \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 3y^2 = 2(x+y)(x-y) - (x+y)^2 + 3y^2$. By traversing the graph once from top to bottom, we efficiently obtain all required partial derivatives.

**[01:08:39]** We are computing $\frac{df}{d(\text{everything})}$. These two approaches are symmetrical, dual to each other, and arguably basically the same, but neural networks are not symmetrical. You always have only one output, because you need a single function to minimize. You cannot minimize several functions at once; if you have a million functions, you must first compose them into a single function. So you always have one output and millions of inputs. The first approach scales well for many outputs and few inputs, but our situation is the opposite: real neural networks have many inputs and one output, so we use backpropagation.

**[01:09:44]** In this computation, starting with $\frac{df}{df} = 1$, as we progressed backwards to compute, say, $\frac{df}{dd}$, what gets transmitted from $e$ to $d$? We needed not only $\frac{df}{de}$ (which came from above) but also $\frac{de}{dd}$. To compute $\frac{de}{dd}$, we needed the value of $b$. So we need the values of these intermediate nodes. It is all local: to compute this, I only need the nodes that go into $e$, but I do need their values, not only their derivatives. Backpropagation is therefore two passes through the graph.

**[01:11:14]** First, you do forward propagation to compute and store the values of intermediate nodes — effectively storing two numbers on every edge. Then, backprop computes $\frac{df}{d(\text{everything})}$ for every intermediate node. This is an efficient, straightforward way to compute gradients.

**[01:12:10]** Any deep learning library, like PyTorch, has as its main job to do these two passes through the graph extremely well. These are the key procedures the library must implement; everything else is syntactic sugar. The many classes for different layers and components are only needed to set up the graph. They are run once and need not be hyper-efficient. The meat of everything is computing the gradient along the graph. PyTorch, TensorFlow, and similar are fundamentally automatic differentiation libraries — deep learning is almost an afterthought. The forward and backward propagation procedures must be extremely efficient; the rest can be done in any way.

**[01:15:01]** To see how this applies to neural networks, consider the inputs. The default answer is that the input is $X$ (e.g., an image). But as a computational graph, a network has two sorts of inputs: fixed inputs we do not optimize (the data, like images and correct labels — the $x$'s and $y$'s) and the weights. The weights are also inputs to the computational graph; they are not computed from anything else, they are variables in the huge formula. During training, we are interested in gradients with respect to the weights. In the earlier example, $x$ and $y$ were both weights, and we needed their gradients.

**[01:17:35]** Let me motivate and introduce stochastic gradient descent. After the break we will discuss the actual algorithms. Many problems in practical deep learning stem from the use of gradient descent. Gradient descent is a first-order algorithm — it comes from a first-order approximation to the loss. Given $L(w)$ at point $w_k$, the Taylor series is $L(w_k) + (w - w_k)^T \nabla L(w_k)$. Gradient descent stops here: the gradient is the direction of steepest increase, so the opposite direction is the steepest decrease, and we go there to minimize.

**[01:18:13]** But we have had centuries of optimization theory; gradient descent could have been done by Newton and Leibniz, and we have since developed more involved, better techniques. The most straightforward improvement would be to go one step further in the Taylor series: include the second derivative term, $\frac{1}{2}(w - w_k)^T H (w - w_k)$. Now we approximate with a quadratic surface. Approximating by a linear surface is naive: if we look at a complicated function and only use the derivative, it simply points in a direction in one dimension.

**[01:21:04]** The gradient gives direction, but how far should you go? That’s the learning rate or step size, and it’s a central problem of gradient descent. If you go one step further and approximate your difficult function locally with a quadratic surface (a parabola in 1D), you can go directly to the minimum of that quadratic. Taking the gradient of the quadratic approximation $G_k + H_k (W - W_k)$—where $G_k$ is the gradient at step $k$ and $H_k$ is the Hessian matrix of second derivatives—and setting it to zero gives $W = W_k - H_k^{-1} G_k$. This update eliminates the step‑size problem entirely.

**[01:23:29]** This procedure—Newton’s method or second‑order optimization—is far more robust, rarely sends you to bad parts of the search space, converges in many fewer iterations, and is better all around. But we can’t use it for deep networks: $W$ is a vector of a million numbers, so $H_k$ is a million‑by‑million matrix. We would need to compute and invert it, which is computationally impossible.

**[01:24:57]** In classical optimization theory (1960s–80s), people faced similar difficulties with second‑order methods and developed quasi‑Newton algorithms as approximations. The most famous is LBFGS (limited‑memory BFGS, where BFGS stands for Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb, and Shanno). It maintains an approximate inverse Hessian update using only the sequence of $W$ vectors and gradient vectors, avoiding million‑by‑million matrices. It’s a low‑rank approximation that is quick and works only at the level of gradients, achieving second‑order–like behavior with relatively low complexity.

**[01:27:13]** So why don’t we do that in deep learning? We would be very happy to use Newton’s method or LBFGS, but we can’t. The reason is that we cannot even compute the true gradient—or we can only compute it once, not at every step.

**[01:28:26]** Why? Because the function we minimize is a huge average (or sum) of loss functions over the entire training set: $L(W) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n L(x_n, y_n, W)$. The neural network computes the loss on a single sample; to get the true objective or its gradient $G_k$, you would need to process the whole dataset every step, which is obviously impractical. So the actual setting we solve is not standard gradient descent, but stochastic gradient descent. I’ve written the losses as averages to emphasize that we can view the loss function as an expectation.

**[1:31:42]** The loss function is an expectation over the uniform distribution of the data set, meaning $(x, y)$ pairs are taken uniformly at random. Since the expectation is the average weighted by probabilities, taking samples with equal probabilities gives the average. So our function is an expectation of a per-example loss with respect to $x$ and $y$, and we want to minimize it with respect to $w$.

**[1:32:34]** This is stochastic optimization. If we cannot compute the expectation directly, the only principled approach is to approximate it with a sample. In practice, on every step we do not use the true gradient $g_k$ (the expectation of the gradients of $\ell$) but an approximation $\hat{g}_k = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \nabla \ell(x_i, y_i, w)$. When training complicated models, we do not compute the loss over the whole data set; we go through mini-batches. Formally, a mini-batch is a sample from the data set. So mathematically, stochastic gradient descent approximates the true, expensive gradient with a sampled gradient. The mean is correct—it is unbiased—and the approximation converges; it has many desirable theoretical properties and is as good as we can hope for.

**[1:35:53]** Even though the approximation is good in theory and we do reach a neighborhood of a minimum with SGD, in practice it is a fairly poor approximation. Data set sizes are in the millions or more, while mini-batch sizes are tiny—often 4 or 8—because that many copies of the network must fit in GPU memory. We are approximating the average of a million numbers with just a handful, so the variance remains large. This variance kills algorithms like L‑BFGS, because those methods also rely on approximations that require very accurate gradient estimates. Replacing the true gradients with highly noisy sampled versions does not work. An interesting and practical open problem in deep learning is whether we can modify quasi‑Newton methods to use stochastic gradient estimates and still converge. Despite much effort, no solution has emerged.

**[1:38:47]** The update formula is $w_{k+1} = w_k - \alpha \hat{g}_k$, where $\alpha$ is the learning rate. This is stochastic gradient descent in its simplest form. It is not the end of the story: there have been many modifications and improvements of this idea, and they lie at the very heart of deep learning.

**[2:01:17]** We stopped at the notion of stochastic gradient descent. Before proceeding to SGD variations, I want to show a few intuitive pictures and results. Classical optimization theory has analyzed SGD in great detail. Much of classical optimization centers on one-dimensional line search: once we know the gradient direction $g_k$ at iterate $w_k$, we still need to decide how far to move in that direction. Many classical results, such as Wolfe's rule and others, address this exact problem. These results do not carry over easily to SGD, but certain insights do.

What does classical theory say about SGD specifically? If you look at its trajectory, you first go quickly toward a neighborhood of the optimum. The initial part of the trajectory heads in the correct direction. However, when you arrive near the optimum, you cannot stop and converge if the step size is kept constant. Because the true gradient at the optimum is zero, but the stochastic gradient estimate is random and almost surely nonzero, so you overshoot and oscillate around the optimum. You never know the exact optimum, only a neighborhood.

With a little math you can derive an interesting estimate for convergence. After $k$ iterations, the expected function value (at a suitable averaged point) satisfies

\[
\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{x}_k)] - f^* \le \frac{R^2 + \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i^2 \|g_i\|^2}{2 \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i},
\]

where $R^2$ is the initial squared distance to the optimum, $\alpha_i$ are the step sizes, and $g_i$ are the stochastic gradient samples. If the stochastic gradients are bounded, this formula fully explains SGD's behavior.

For the special case of a constant step size $\alpha_i = h$, the bound splits into two terms: the first is $R^2/(hk)$, which vanishes as $k \to \infty$, and the second is a constant (involving $h$ and the gradient norms) that does not depend on $k$. Hence, however far we start, we rapidly approach the optimum, but the noise prevents exact convergence—a constant error remains. To force convergence, we need the denominator $\sum \alpha_i$ to diverge while the numerator $\sum \alpha_i^2$ stays bounded; that is the classic condition for decaying step sizes.

**[2:10:07]** One practical conclusion from the convergence analysis is that to make SGD converge to an optimum, the step sizes $\alpha_k$ should be reduced so that $\sum_{k} \alpha_k$ diverges but $\sum_{k} \alpha_k^2$ converges. The simplest such schedule is $\alpha_k = 1/k$; then SGD converges. In practice, however, the bound involves $g^2$, the variance of the sampled gradients, which is unknown. So while the analysis provides qualitative guidance, it does not yield precise step-size estimates.

**[2:12:16]** Now, in the second part of the class, we turn to improvements and variations of SGD. An important problem can be illustrated with the simplest possible quadratic function: $f(x,y) = x^2 + \rho y^2$. The minimum is at the origin, the function is convex, and a second-order method like Newton's would converge in one step. For gradient descent, however, the performance depends crucially on $\rho$.

**[2:15:02]** Setting $\rho = 1/2$ yields nearly circular contours. Gradient descent works well: a learning rate of $0.01$ is too slow, but larger rates like $0.5$ go straight to the minimum. (This is plain gradient descent, not stochastic; there are no mini‑batches.)

**[2:16:13]** Reducing $\rho$ to $1/20$ makes the coordinates imbalanced: $y$ changes 50 times slower than $x$. The range of useful learning rates shrinks; $0.1$ is now too slow, and only $0.5$ reaches zero within 200 iterations — surprisingly slow for such a simple function. As $\rho$ decreases further, the situation worsens. One might think to increase the learning rate, but for this quadratic the maximum stable step size is limited by the steepest direction (the $x$‑axis), so one cannot exceed $1$. Consequently, gradient descent becomes prohibitively slow on ill‑conditioned problems.

**[2:18:15]** Even a little bit then the x coordinate starts to diverge, because along the x coordinate the coefficient is still one. The problem is not that y changes slowly in absolute values—that wouldn't be a problem. The problem is that x and y change at very different rates, they have very different scales. At this point, even a 500–200 fold difference between them, there is already no good learning rate for gradient descent; it either diverges or takes hopelessly long to go anywhere. With 2000 iterations we might get there, but 2000 iterations of gradient descent for a quadratic function starting from 0.1 and 1.5 going to 0 does not make any sense.

**[2:20:33]** So here is the problem: when different variables in your optimization function begin to differ in scale, begin to have different rates at which we should move along them, everything kind of fails. Gradient descent cannot have a unified rate. The ideas we will discuss to solve this are still real and useful. First, set up the notation: from $x_{k-1}$ to $x_k$, I define the update $u_{k-1} = x_k - x_{k-1}$. At $x_k$ I have a gradient estimate $g_k$. In classical SGD or GD, $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha g_k$, and $u_k$ is just $-\alpha g_k$. Idea number one: momentum. Taking a physical analogy, gradient descent is like a ball rolling down a slope. If the slope suddenly changes, the ball keeps going along its current velocity for a while due to momentum. In the same way, we not only move along the gradient, but we also conserve a bit of the previous update.

**[2:25:14]** The momentum method adds a portion of the previous update, $\gamma u_{k-1}$, to the gradient step. The final update is $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha \nabla f(x_k) + \gamma u_{k-1}$. In practice, we keep a very large part of the momentum: $\gamma$ is typically $0.95$ or $0.99$, maybe even more, so there is little friction, and the iteration keeps moving in the same direction.

**[2:26:33]** Momentum keeps the ball rolling forward along a direction if the gradient continues to point that way, gradually picking up speed. In the ravine picture, along the steep $x$-direction the ball oscillates and quickly stabilizes near zero because the slope changes sign. Along the shallow $y$-direction it speeds up, steadily descending the long, gentle slope. This is the motivation for momentum.

**[2:28:28]** The second approach, a small but theoretically and practically important modification, is Nesterov accelerated gradients (or Nesterov's method). Yuri Nesterov, a Soviet mathematician, invented it in the 1980s. The idea is simple: when we stand at $x_k$, we already know the momentum part of the next update, so instead of computing the gradient at $x_k$, we go to the lookahead point $x_k + \gamma u_{k-1}$ and compute the gradient there. Then the update becomes $x_{k+1} = x_k + \gamma u_{k-1} - \alpha \nabla f(x_k + \gamma u_{k-1})$.

**[02:31:03]** What is the motivation behind this? First, there is some beautiful mathematics: gradient descent with this method starts to converge asymptotically faster — some asymptotics change when you go from regular momentum to Nesterov momentum — but we will not go there. The intuitive motivation is that Nesterov's gradients do a look-ahead: they look forward in time, try to predict what will happen, and update based on the future, not the present. So if we are on a slope and a ball rolls down, picking up speed, regular momentum would continue speeding up because the slope is still in that direction, overshooting up the opposite wall. But with Nesterov momentum, you look ahead and see that the gradient now points in the opposite direction, so you slow down before the turn — like a good driver anticipating the curve, not a passively rolling point. That is what makes it better.

**[02:33:27]** Looking at our example, I have added gradient descent with momentum (orange line) and gradient descent with Nesterov momentum (green line) alongside regular gradient descent. You can see how the oscillations along the x-axis are much more contained with Nesterov's method. Again, this is exactly the same effect: along the x-axis, the slope looks like this, and a material point would oscillate, but Nesterov's momentum looks ahead and starts slowing before the turn, like a good driver.

**[02:34:35]** However, the idea did not stop there. Both the classical momentum method (dating to the 1960s) and Nesterov's gradients (1980s) come from classical optimization theory; they are not special to deep learning. But when you consider the same kind of problems in the context of deep learning, you see the following: everything we have done so far operates in the generic Euclidean space of the parameter vector $x$. For the method of momentum, all directions are equal — it does not treat $x_1$ differently from $x_2$ or $x_{10}$; it just adds up vectors. But it turns out you can do even better if you treat different components of $x$ differently, picking a separate learning rate for each component. When you do this, it is called adaptive gradient descent (and now almost always stochastic gradient descent, because we are in deep learning territory).

**[2:36:30]** These are called adaptive stochastic gradient descent (SGD) variations; they are the optimization algorithms used in deep learning practice. I will walk through a line of algorithms culminating in Adam, but to get to Adam we need to discuss some ideas along the way.

**[2:37:05]** The first variation is AdaGrad (adaptive gradient), the original adaptive SGD version. The idea is: on a shallow slope, you should move quickly; on a steep slope, the local optimum is close, so you should slow down to converge carefully.

**[2:38:28]** AdaGrad implements this with a simple update rule for each component $i$:
$$x_{k+1}^{(i)} = x_k^{(i)} - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{G_{k,i} + \epsilon}} g_{k,i},$$
where $G_{k,i} = G_{k-1,i} + g_{k,i}^2$ accumulates the squared gradients. The learning rate is now adaptive: $\alpha/\sqrt{G_{k,i} + \epsilon}$. $G$ grows with the history of gradients; if gradients are large, $G$ becomes large and the effective learning rate shrinks. On a shallow slope, $g_k$ is small, so the denominator grows slowly, and we continue walking quickly. When we encounter a steep slope, the large $g_k$ inflates $G$, shrinking the learning rate and slowing us down—exactly the desired behavior.

**[2:40:52]** The obvious drawback is that the denominator $G_{k,i}$ grows monotonically with time. Once we have seen many steps, the learning rate becomes extremely small, and learning stops.

**[02:41:30]** If our optimization landscape has a steep slope and then becomes shallow again, there is no way to speed back up. The denominator in AdaGrad only grows, so when the gradients become small again we are doomed to very slow progress. This is a bad situation.

**[02:42:07]** The obvious modification is to not use the sum of all past gradients but some recent history. If recent gradients have been large we go slow, but if they have been small for a while we can speed up again. A sliding window would be expensive because you must store the whole window. Instead, we use an exponential moving average (EMA). The update for the accumulator $G_k$ is $G_k = \gamma G_{k-1} + (1 - \gamma) g_k^2$, a convex combination of the previous value and the new squared gradient. Expanding this, each past squared gradient is weighted by powers of $\gamma$, i.e., exponentially decreasing coefficients. So recent history has high weight, while distant history is exponentially forgotten. Unlike a sliding window, we only need to store one scalar per parameter, making it memory efficient.

**[02:45:03]** If you take AdaGrad and replace the sum of squared gradients with this EMA, you obtain an algorithm known as RMSprop. It is a standard approach for stochastic gradient descent, popularized by Geoffrey Hinton. But the story does not end there; we will now move almost directly to Adam.

**[02:45:56]** Let me explain one more angle of looking at it. I think it's a pretty fun and instructive angle, although the algorithm that results from it wasn't very good and isn't really used, but the motivation behind it is really nice.

**[02:46:23]** We already discussed gradient descent as points rolling down surfaces. So why don't we keep looking at it from this physics perspective, pursuing this metaphor of points rolling downhill? It's a very direct correspondence; the only difference is that balls roll in continuous time while we are in discrete time, but otherwise it's the exact same process.

**[02:47:12]** Let's look at the measurement units of what is going on. Start with the gradient, because it's more natural. I want my gradient to be like velocity; my intuition is that derivatives are speeds. So say $g$ is measured in meters per second. $g = \frac{df}{dx}$, so $x$ is measured in seconds and $f(x)$ is measured in meters. The function is how far the point has gone, $x$ is the elapsed time, and $g$ is the velocity. Now look at the gradient descent update: $x \leftarrow x - \alpha g$. $x$ is in seconds, $\alpha$ is a dimensionless constant, and $g$ is meters per second. We are subtracting velocity from time, which makes no physical sense. Mathematically, this means the scales of the variables can be very different. It can happen that your axes are large in absolute value and $g$ is relatively small, requiring a huge $\alpha$ to adjust, or the other way around. So $g$ is not scaled well to $x$; it's our job to scale it. Physically, the dimensions are wrong, the units do not match.

**[02:49:44]** In momentum, it's the same. What happens in AdaGrad? In AdaGrad we have $x \leftarrow x - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{G}} g$, where $G$ is the sum of small $g$ squared. The units: $\alpha$ is dimensionless, $G$ has units $(\text{m/s})^2$, so the term becomes dimensionless. Now we are subtracting a dimensionless constant from time, which also doesn't sound right. Notice that Newton's method, a second-order optimization, does not have this problem. The update is $x \leftarrow x - H^{-1} g$. $H$ is the matrix of second derivatives—accelerations—so its units are $\text{m/s}^2$. Its inverse is $\text{s}^2/\text{m}$, times $g$ ($\text{m/s}$) yields seconds, matching $x$. So the units actually match. Mathematically, Newton's method scales correctly, giving you the local step size you actually want. This is one good illustration of why second-order techniques are better: their dimensions match and they scale automatically.

**[02:52:00]** Out of this idea came a method called AdaDelta, which tried to get the proper units. Basically, it was AdaGrad but $\alpha$ was multiplied by something like the square root of $u$, where $u$ was something like the sum of updates squared. It wasn't a very good algorithm; it didn't catch on. So you don't have to study AdaDelta, but I think the basic idea is very nice.

**[02:52:36]** Let's turn to the real thing: the optimization algorithm people still use quite often, ADAM. Although alternatives exist, ADAM remains the most popular in deep learning. It is very similar to RMSprop with slight modifications.

**[02:53:19]** The update rule is $x_{k+1,i} = x_{k,i} - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{G_{k,i} + \epsilon}} m_{k,i}$, where $G_{k,i} = \beta_2 G_{k-1,i} + (1-\beta_2) g_{k,i}^2$ is an exponential moving average of squared gradients, and $m_{k,i} = \beta_1 m_{k-1,i} + (1-\beta_1) g_{k,i}$ is an exponential moving average of the gradients themselves. So the only difference from RMSprop is that we also smooth the gradients directly, reducing their variance across mini-batches and making them less noisy. This smoothing is what makes ADAM work so well.

**[02:55:14]** ADAM looks like it has many hyperparameters ($\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, $\epsilon$), but it is robust. You can simply use the standard recommended values from the original paper—something like $\beta_2 = 0.995$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$—without tweaking, and it will work fine.

**[02:56:07]** ADAM is a very good algorithm, still used today. The whole point of adaptive methods is to treat different components of the parameter vector differently; they shine in high dimensions where some dimensions have one kind of optimization landscape and others another. This cannot be illustrated in 2D—on a plane, you won't see a meaningful difference between ADAM and RMSprop.

**[02:57:15]** You will see such high-dimensional examples in the practical part. Classical optimization test functions like Beale's or Rosenbrock's function are complicated polynomials often used as testbeds, but 2D pictures of them do not show the true power of adaptive methods.

**[2:58:04]** Some complicated polynomial—you don't have to memorize it, it's just a set of constants tweaked to produce an interesting optimization landscape. There's a minimum over here, but around it you have a very steep slope (contour lines close together) that suddenly becomes very shallow. It's easy to go quickly down the steep part, but then you need to recognize that the slope is now shallow and speed up, which some algorithms cannot do. This is a good test example, though I don't think it really illustrates the difference between Adam and other adaptive variants.

**[2:58:58]** Here's what SGD does. Unless the learning rate is so high that everything diverges, SGD goes in the correct direction. The faster you go the closer you get, but too fast risks divergence; with SGD it's always about finding the best learning rate.

**[2:59:30]** If you add momentum, you see exactly the effects we discussed. Standard SGD still has a long way to go. SGD with momentum almost left the basin of this minimum—it nearly failed, but came back, and now it's far and away the optimum; it disappeared on the right and hasn't returned in 200 steps. Nesterov's momentum is already coming back—it's not quite there yet but is clearly the best of the three and will arrive soon.

**[3:00:17]** As for the adaptive algorithms we discussed, like SGD and AdaDelta, they kind of do better; no real difference here. AdaDelta might seem worse, but you shouldn't draw conclusions from this—AdaDelta and Adam both just do a good job, so there is no significant difference. But this is what optimization looks like in dimension 2; it is not a good guide to what optimization looks like in dimension 100, but unfortunately I cannot plot dimension 100 for you.

**[3:00:59]** To wrap up, I have a couple more things to mention. One is a practical version of Adam that you can actually encounter in deep learning practice. It also comes from a very interesting motivation, which is a different way of doing regularization. Back in the 1980s and 1990s, classical neural networks typically used weight decay. What is the difference between the regularization we discussed yesterday and weight decay? In standard regularization, the loss function is $L(W)$ plus something like $\alpha$ times a regularizer; you then minimize that regularized loss. This is very easy in deep learning—it's just a slight modification of the loss function, algorithms don't change, and you only add a couple of nodes to the computational graph. Classical neural networks did it differently. Weight decay works like this: when you go from $w_k$ to $w_{k+1}$ in gradient descent, before adding the gradient you shrink the current weights by multiplying them by a constant less than 1, and then you add or subtract the current gradient.

**[3:03:38]** Weight decay is so called because the weights decay a little before the gradient step. The update can be written $W_k = W_{k-1} - \gamma W_{k-1} - \alpha \nabla L(W_{k-1})$, where $\gamma$ is the decay factor and $\alpha$ the learning rate. Pulling out $W_{k-1}$ gives $W_k = W_{k-1} - \alpha\bigl(\nabla L(W_{k-1}) + \frac{\gamma}{\alpha} W_{k-1}\bigr)$, which is exactly gradient descent on the regularized loss $L(W) + \frac{\gamma}{2\alpha}\|W\|^2$ evaluated at $W_{k-1}$. Thus weight decay is completely equivalent to L2 regularization with a suitable constant.

**[3:05:32]** In standard gradient descent this equivalence holds, and weight decay was initially ignored in deep learning. But for adaptive methods like Adam, which use momentum and exponential moving averages of squared gradients, the equivalence breaks: the L2 regularization term interacts with the adaptive scaling, whereas weight decay applies a constant multiplicative shrink. Researchers carefully worked through the Adam update and derived a small modification that restores the weight‑decay behavior, yielding the popular AdamW algorithm.

**[3:07:04]** Research has continued beyond Adam and AdamW. Recently developed algorithms include Lion and Muon; one of them was discovered through automated search (similar to how the Swish activation was found), and another exploits transformer properties. Still, in 2026 the standard choices remain Adam, AdamW, or classical SGD—the latter is still very effective with a well‑tuned learning rate.

**[3:08:15]** We covered Nesterov momentum, AdaGrad, AdaDelta, RMSProp, Adam, and NAdam (Adam with Nesterov momentum).

**[3:09:11]** Two last stories illustrate the same point. The first is Superconvergence. In 2017 a famous paper proposed a cyclic learning‑rate schedule: slow down, then speed up again, producing large updates. Experiments showed convergence to similar results 10–20× faster, and it caused a buzz, but today it is not universally accepted. Cyclical learning rates still exist, but they are not a standard tool.

**[3:10:46]** The second is AMSGrad. The Adam paper included a convergence proof with an estimated rate, but the proof contained a mistake. The AMSGrad authors found the error, gave counterexamples where Adam diverges, and proposed a fix—replacing a sum with a maximum—that guarantees convergence. They won the ICLR 2018 Best Paper Award. Despite having a provably correct algorithm, nobody uses AMSGrad in practice; it tends to perform the same as Adam or worse. The conclusion is that even ideas confirmed by experiments and correct theory are not enough to displace established practice. The field is deeply applied, and people ultimately use what works—and Adam still works.
