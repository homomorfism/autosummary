# Generative Adversarial Networks

## Introduction to GANs

### GAN Overview

- Generative adversarial networks (GAN) consist of a **generator** and a **discriminator**, competing over the true data distribution $p_\text{data}$ and the model distribution $p_g$.
- Ideally the training proceeds as follows (illustrated by Geitgey, 2017):
  - At first the generator is bad and the discriminator is also untrained.
  - First train the discriminator a little, then the generator must improve to catch up.
  - They keep alternating until both converge.

### Formal Definition

The generator is a function  
$$G = G(z; \theta_g) : Z \to X,$$
and the discriminator is a function  
$$D = D(x; \theta_d) : X \to [0, 1].$$

The discriminator solves a binary classification problem:
$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g(x)}[\log(1 - D(x))],$$
where $p_g(x) = G_{z \sim p_z(z)}$ is the distribution generated by $G$; that is, we take two mini-batches, with labels $0$ from the generator and labels $1$ from the real data.

The generator learns to fool the discriminator, i.e., to minimize
$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g(x)}[\log(1 - D(x))] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))].$$

Combining the two yields the minimax game:
$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G),$$
where
$$V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))].$$

### Theoretical Optimality

- For a fixed generator $G$, the optimal discriminator is
  $$D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_\text{data}(x)}{p_\text{data}(x) + p_g(x)}.$$
  (Exercise: try to prove this.)
- The global minimum of the criterion is attained if and only if $p_g = p_\text{data}$.
- At the optimal discriminator, the criterion is equivalent to the **Jensen–Shannon divergence**:
  $$\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p_\text{data} \Big\| \frac{p_\text{data} + p_g}{2}\right) + \operatorname{KL}\!\left(p_g \Big\| \frac{p_\text{data} + p_g}{2}\right).$$

### Practical Generator Loss

Minimizing $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$ has a drawback:
- In a classifier, cross-entropy does not saturate when the answer is wrong, and saturates when the answer is right – which is good.
- Here, when the discriminator works well, its gradient vanishes, and consequently the generator gradient also vanishes.
- Therefore, in practice we minimize
  $$-\mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log D(G(z))],$$
  i.e., we **maximize** the probability that $D$ made a mistake, rather than minimizing the probability of its correct answer.

### Training Algorithm

Training is essentially an alternating procedure similar to the EM algorithm:
1. Fix $G$; update the discriminator with the loss  
   $$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g(x)}[\log(1 - D(x))].$$
2. Fix $D$; update the generator with the loss  
   $$-\mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log D(G(z))].$$

## DCGAN – Deep Convolutional GAN

DCGAN (Radford et al., 2016) introduced several tricks that made training more stable:
- Strided convolutions instead of pooling.
- Batch normalisation everywhere.
- Removal of fully connected layers.
- Adam optimiser.

Results on LSUN bedrooms:
- After one epoch: coarse room structures appear.
- After five epochs: much sharper, realistic bedrooms.

Additional explorations:
- Walking through the feature space produces smooth transitions.
- Removing filters that detect windows removes windows from generated images.
- Vector arithmetic in the latent space (as in word2vec) – e.g., “man with glasses” minus “man” plus “woman” yields “woman with glasses”.
- Application to pixel art from NES games (Geitgey, 2017).

Limitations:
- Problems with counting objects.
- Problems with perspective.

## Progressive Growing GAN (ProGAN)

A major breakthrough came from NVIDIA (Karras et al., 2017) with **Progressive Growing GAN (ProGAN)**:
- At each stage, the GAN is trained to generate images four times larger, starting from $4 \times 4$.
- The resolution is gradually increased up to $1024 \times 1024$.
- New layers are added progressively; the discriminator remains roughly symmetric.

This approach saves training time because more data can be shown in the same wall‑clock time. (See video demonstrations: *YouTube links*.)

For some categories the resolution is stopped at $256 \times 256$, which already looks very good. The authors compare loss functions of **Least Squares GAN** and **Wasserstein GAN**, which leads to the next topic.

## Loss Functions in GANs

### Least Squares GAN (LSGAN)

(Mao et al., 2016) **Least Squares GAN** addresses the saturation problem of the Jensen–Shannon divergence when $p_g$ is far from $p_\text{data}$. Standard GANs use a discriminator with a logistic sigmoid, which leads to vanishing gradients. LSGAN replaces it with a quadratic loss:

- Discriminator loss:
  $$\min_D V_{\text{LSGAN}}(D) = \frac12 \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}}[(D(x) - b)^2] + \frac12 \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[(D(G(z)) - a)^2],$$
- Generator loss:
  $$\min_G V_{\text{LSGAN}}(G) = \frac12 \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[(D(G(z)) - c)^2].$$

Here the discriminator learns to output $a$ for fakes and $b$ for real data, while the generator wants the discriminator to output $c$ on fakes. Usually one sets $a = 0$, $b = c = 1$.

LSGAN is more robust to architectural changes, suffers less from mode collapse, and the quadratic loss function is now widely used.

### InfoGAN

(Chen et al., 2016) **InfoGAN: Interpretable Representation Learning by Information Maximizing GANs** adds **disentanglement** so that different features become meaningful. In ordinary GANs the input is simply a noise vector $z$. InfoGAN splits it into two parts:
- $z$ – truly incompressible noise, the source of entropy;
- $c = c_1 c_2 \dots c_L$ – a **latent code**.

The generator becomes $G(z, c)$, but an extra objective is needed so that $G$ cannot simply ignore $c$. InfoGAN therefore requires high mutual information $I(c, G(z, c))$, i.e., the distribution $G(z, c)$ should retain a lot of $c$.

A variational lower bound on the mutual information is parameterised with a neural network $Q$ (usually almost the same as $D$), using the reparametrisation trick.

Example on MNIST with $c = c_1 c_2 c_3$:
- $c_1$ – discrete label (10 categories, uniform prior) **without supervision**;
- $c_2, c_3 \sim U[-1,1]$ control continuous factors.

Using only the code $c_1$, a classifier achieves a 5% error on MNIST without any labels.

Further results: 3D faces, 3D chairs.

### Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)

(Arjovsky et al., 2017) **Wasserstein GAN** re-examines what it means to learn a probability distribution.

- Training by minimising $\operatorname{KL}(p_\text{data} \| p_\text{model})$ requires that $p_\text{model}$ exist everywhere; when the model distribution is concentrated on low‑dimensional submanifolds, the supports may not overlap, making $\operatorname{KL}$ undefined (infinite).
- In practice, one adds noise (e.g., Gaussian) to make the KL well‑defined, but this noise makes generated samples blurry.
- For generative models, a distance that behaves well even on low‑dimensional supports is needed.

**Earth Mover Distance** (Wasserstein‑1):
$$W(p_\text{data}, p_\text{model}) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(p_\text{data}, p_\text{model})} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \gamma}\big[\|x - y\|\big],$$
where $\Pi(p_\text{data}, p_\text{model})$ is the set of joint distributions $\gamma(x, y)$ whose marginals are $p_\text{data}$ and $p_\text{model}$. Informally, $\gamma(x, y)$ shows how much “mass” must be moved from $x$ to $y$ to transform $p_\text{data}$ into $p_\text{model}$.

**Example:** consider $Z \sim U[0,1]$, $\mathbb{P}_0$ – the distribution of $(0, Z)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $g_\theta(z) = (\theta, z)$. As $\theta \to 0$, only the EM distance converges continuously, while other distances (like Jensen–Shannon or KL) behave pathologically. This property is crucial for learning distributions concentrated on submanifolds.

The Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality gives an equivalent formulation:
$$W(p_\text{data}, p_\text{model}) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1} \Big(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{model}}[f(x)]\Big),$$
where the supremum is taken over all **1‑Lipschitz** functions.

Parametrising with neural networks: a critic $f_w$ (instead of a discriminator) and generator $g_\theta$.
- For fixed $g_\theta$, train $f_w$ to maximise $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\text{data}}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\text{model}}[f(x)]$.
- For fixed $f_w$, the gradient for the generator is
  $$\nabla_\theta W = -\mathbb{E}_{z \sim Z}\!\big[\nabla_\theta f_w(g_\theta(z))\big].$$

To enforce the Lipschitz constraint, one can simply apply **weight clipping** to the gradients.

Training algorithm:
1. Sample a batch from real data and a batch from the generator.
2. Train the critic $f_w$ (often multiple iterations) with the above objective and weight clipping.
3. Train the generator with the gradient above.

WGAN allows the critic to be trained to convergence, unlike the discriminator in an ordinary GAN. The loss value strongly correlates with sample quality. WGAN is also more robust to architectural changes: even when batchnorm is removed or fewer filters are used, it still produces reasonable results.

**Improved WGAN (WGAN‑GP)** (Gulrajani et al., 2017): weight clipping can lead to problems (poor higher‑moment learning, exploding/vanishing gradients). A better solution is a soft gradient penalty regulariser:
$$\lambda \,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x} \sim \mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}}\!\big[(\|\nabla_{\hat{x}} D(\hat{x})\|_2 - 1)^2\big].$$

## Self‑Attention GAN (SAGAN) and BigGAN

(Zhang et al., 2019) **Self‑Attention GAN (SAGAN)** tackles the locality problem of convolutional generators. Self‑attention adapted from the Transformer is applied to images. The generator can attend to distant regions; visualisations of the attention maps confirm that it looks far away when generating an image.

SAGAN already produces good results. The next step, **BigGAN** (Brock et al., 2019), pushed the Inception score threefold using SAGAN with extra architectural tricks.

### Inception Score

Assessing image quality is difficult. The **Inception Score** (Salimans et al., 2016) works as follows:
- Take generated images $x$.
- Apply the standard Inception classifier to obtain the conditional label distribution $p(y \mid x)$.
- We want $p(y \mid x)$ to have low entropy (sharp labels) and at the same time we want diversity, i.e., the marginal $p(y) = \int p(y \mid x = G(z))\,dz$ should have high entropy.
- The score is $\exp(\mathbb{E}_x \operatorname{KL}(p(y \mid x) \| p(y)))$.

It is difficult to use this for training, but it works well for evaluation.

## GAN‑based Architectures

### Adversarial Autoencoders (AAE)

(Makhzani et al., 2015) **Adversarial Autoencoders**: a discriminator forces the distribution of the encoder’s hidden features to match a given prior $p(z)$.

Making the distributions conditional enables semi‑supervised learning.

Applications:
- (Kadurin et al., 2017) – AAE for generating molecules in oncology.
- (Kadurin et al., 2018) – druGAN for molecule generation (comparison with VAE).
- (Polikovsky et al., 2018) – MOSES, a platform for comparing generative models for molecules.

### Conditional GANs (cGAN)

(Mirza and Osindero, 2014) **Conditional GANs**: the generator receives a random vector together with an input vector (the condition).

Examples:
- (Antipov et al., 2017) – face aging with a conditional GAN.
- (Ledig et al., 2017) – ESRGAN for super‑resolution.

### Stacked GANs

(Huang et al., 2017) **Stacked Generative Adversarial Networks (SGAN)** chain several GANs sequentially.
- Each generator $G_i$ learns lower‑level representations, essentially inverting a deep encoder $E_i$.

New loss functions:
- **Conditional loss** – we train $\hat{h}_i$ conditioned on $h_{i+1}$, and to prevent the generator from ignoring the condition, $h_{i+1}$ must be reconstructed through the corresponding encoder:
  $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{cond}} = \mathbb{E}_{h_{i+1} \sim p_\text{data},\, z_i \sim p_{z_i}}\!\big[d\big(E_i(G_i(h_{i+1}, z_i)),\, h_{i+1}\big)\big].$$
- **Entropy loss** – we also need $G_i$ not to ignore the noise $z$, i.e., we must add diversity. This is done by maximising entropy:
  $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}} = \mathbb{E}_{z_i \sim p_{z_i}}\!\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}_i \sim G_i(\hat{h}_i \mid z_i)}\big[-\log Q_i(z_i \mid \hat{h}_i)\big]\Big],$$
  where $Q_i$ is a neural‑network‑parametrised approximation to the posterior $p_i(z_i \mid \hat{h}_i)$, a variational bound.

SGAN outperforms its predecessors, though progressive growing (ProGAN) is even better.
