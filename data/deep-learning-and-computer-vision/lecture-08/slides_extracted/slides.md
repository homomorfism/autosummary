## Slide 7

GAN
• Formally, the generator is a function
𝐺= 𝐺(z; 𝜃𝑔) ∶𝑍→𝑋,
and the discriminator is a function
𝐷= 𝐷(x; 𝜃𝑑) ∶𝑋→[0, 1].
• The objective function for the discriminator is binary
classification:
Ex∼𝑝data(x) [log 𝐷(x)] + Ex∼𝑝𝑔(x) [log(1 −𝐷(x))] ,
where 𝑝𝑔(x) = 𝐺z∼𝑝𝑧(z) is the distribution generated by 𝐺; that
is, we take two mini-batches, with labels 0 from the generator
and labels 1 from the data.

## Slide 11

GAN
• What is wrong with minimizing E𝑧∼𝑝𝑧(𝑧)[log(1 −𝐷(𝐺(𝑧)))]?
• cross-entropy is good in a classifier: if the answer is wrong, it does
not saturate, and if it is right, it saturates and does not change;
• here it turns out that when the discriminator works well, its
gradient vanishes...
• ...and the generator gradient vanishes too!
• That is why in practice we minimize
−E𝑧∼𝑝𝑧(𝑧)[log 𝐷(𝐺(z))],
i.e. we maximize the probability that 𝐷made a mistake, rather
than minimizing the probability of its correct answer.
4

## Slide 47

LSGAN
• (Mao et al., 2016): Least Squares GAN (LSGAN)
• The problem with ordinary GANs: the loss function
(Jensen-Shannon divergence) saturates when the generator
distribution is far from the correct one, and nothing gets trained.
• This is because the discriminator in an ordinary GAN works like
a classifier with a logistic sigmoid.
11

## Slide 48

LSGAN
• Let’s try switching from a sigmoidal to a quadratic loss function:
min
𝐷𝑉LSGAN(𝐷) = 1
2 𝔼x∼𝑝data [(𝐷(x) −𝑏)2] + 1
2 𝔼z∼𝑝z [(𝐷(𝐺(z)) −𝑎)2] ,
min
𝐺𝑉LSGAN(𝐺) = 1
2 𝔼z∼𝑝z [(𝐷(𝐺(z)) −𝑐)2] ,
i.e. the discriminator learns to answer 𝑎for fakes and 𝑏for real
data, while the generator wants to convince it to answer 𝑐on
fakes. Usually one simply takes 𝑎= 0, 𝑏= 𝑐= 1.

INFOGAN
• (Chen et al., 2016): InfoGAN: Interpretable Representation
Learning by Information Maximizing Generative Adversarial Nets
• An important addition: it would be great to add

• In ordinary GANs we just use a noise vector z. In InfoGAN we
split it into parts:
• z is the truly incompressible noise, the source of entropy;
• c = 𝑐1𝑐2 … 𝑐𝐿is the latent code.
• One can try adding this to an ordinary GAN so that the generator
becomes 𝐺(z, c), but we need to add something so that c
matters and 𝐺cannot simply ignore it.

## Slide 55

WASSERSTEIN GAN
• Wasserstein GAN (Arjovsky et al., 2017):
• what does it mean to learn a probability distribution?
• it means minimizing KL(𝑝data‖𝑝model);
• but for this 𝑝model has to exist, which is not the case if the
distribution is concentrated on low-dimensional submanifolds;
the manifold of 𝑝model is unlikely to intersect substantially with
the manifold of 𝑝data, and KL will be undefined (infinite);
• usually we add noise (Gaussian, for example), all models in ML
have noise;
• but for generative models noise gets in the way, making the
generated samples blurry;
• so usually noise is used for computing ML, but removed during
generation, i.e. noise is wrong, but needed for ML.
13

## Slide 56

WASSERSTEIN GAN
• Wasserstein GAN (Arjovsky et al., 2017): let’s look at other metrics
of closeness between 𝑝data and 𝑝model.
• Earth Mover distance, or Wasserstein-1:
𝑊(𝑝data, 𝑝model) =
inf
𝛾∈Π(𝑝data,𝑝model) 𝔼(x,y)∼𝛾[‖x −y‖] ,
where Π(𝑝data, 𝑝model) is the set of joint distributions 𝛾(x, y)
whose marginals are 𝑝data and 𝑝model.

## Slide 84

STACKED GANS
• It is often useful to chain several GANs in a row.
• (Huang et al., 2017): Stacked Generative Adversarial Networks
• 𝐺𝑖sequentially learns lower-level representations, inverting a
deep network of 𝐸𝑖.
• New loss functions:
• conditional loss – we train̂ h𝑖conditioned on h𝑖+1, and so that the
generator does not ignore the condition, we need h𝑖+1 to be

ℒcond = 𝔼h𝑖+1∼𝑝data,z𝑖∼𝑝z𝑖[𝑑(𝐸𝑖(𝐺𝑖(h𝑖+1, z𝑖)), h𝑖+1)] ;

diversity; for this we maximize entropy:
ℒent = 𝔼z𝑖∼𝑝z𝑖[𝔼̂h𝑖∼𝐺𝑖(̂h𝑖∣z𝑖) [−log 𝑄𝑖(z𝑖∣̂ h𝑖]] ,
where 𝑄𝑖is a neural-network-parametrized approximation for the
posterior distribution 𝑝𝑖(z𝑖∣̂ h𝑖) (a variational bound).
