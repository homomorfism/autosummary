**[01:13]** Yesterday, we discussed learning in a probabilistic Bayesian framework, centered on Bayes' rule—the most important formula in machine learning. We focused on a simple coin toss example. Today we'll start moving toward deep learning by extending this introduction to linear models, which are directly relevant to neural networks.

**[02:29]** Let me continue with a quick reminder: Bayes' rule tells us how to update our beliefs when we receive new evidence. In general terms, $D$ is any evidence, and $\theta$ represents our beliefs or hypotheses about the underlying process. We don't compute these updates consciously, but that's the essence. Yesterday we discussed three main machine learning problems that give rise to optimization: (1) maximize the likelihood $p(D \mid \theta)$ alone, a non-Bayesian method often used with large data or complex models where a prior is infeasible; (2) multiply the likelihood by a prior $p(\theta)$ and maximize the result, which is proportional to the posterior $p(\theta \mid D)$; (3) compute the predictive distribution, which we won't cover today. We'll see concrete examples of the second approach in real models today.

**[04:58]** Yesterday we examined the coin toss example in great detail, going through likelihood, prior, and posterior, finishing with a uniform prior, then discussing more informative priors. If one wants a bell-shaped prior, how to choose? The answer: pick the one that's convenient. This may sound unsatisfactory, but prior choice reflects our beliefs. In more involved cases like linear regression that we will discuss now, the reasoning is the same: we choose the prior for convenience or for desirable properties, not from a principled understanding of the process.

**[06:31]** I want to briefly mention the philosophical underpinning. Old probability books define probability as the limit of relative frequency in infinitely repeatable experiments. This is correct where applicable. But in real life, and especially in machine learning, we often reason about events that are not repeatable. For example, a bookmaker sets odds on Spain winning the FIFA World Cup—a one-off event with different teams each time. Or we may assign probabilities to hypotheses about already determined facts we don't know, such as the authorship of the Odyssey. It makes no sense to talk about infinite repetitions. In such cases, probabilities are not limits of frequencies but degrees of belief—representations of our uncertainty. This is a useful framing for machine learning.

**[09:30]** When we do machine learning, we aren't deriving logical conclusions; we are updating our beliefs. With a good, large dataset, our beliefs become more certain—confidence intervals shrink—but we can never be completely sure, especially with noisy data. The result of combining evidence to infer model parameters is always a probability distribution. This point is often overlooked because in practice we do something else. Let me preview where we are headed.

**[11:13]** In deep learning practice, we compose a loss function: $-\log p(D \mid \theta) - \log p(\theta)$. Minimizing this with respect to the neural network parameters $\theta$ yields $\theta_{\text{MAP}}$, the maximum a posteriori estimate: $\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta} p(\theta \mid D)$. Often, deep learning practitioners stop here and treat the resulting weights as the true parameters. This is an oversimplification: $\theta_{\text{MAP}}$ is only a point estimate, the maximum of a whole distribution.

**[13:04]** In deep learning with huge networks, we usually cannot sample from the posterior, but if we could, sampling multiple parameter sets would show how predictions vary. Sometimes this appears in practice. All of this machinery is about degrees of belief.

**[13:34]** Our original uncertainty about parameters $\Theta$ is captured in the prior distribution $P(\Theta)$. We obtain data $D$, which reduces this uncertainty, leading to the posterior distribution $P(\Theta \mid D)$. Even if we initially know nothing, we express this as a distribution. After updating, our belief about $\Theta$ remains a distribution—an uncertain belief, never reduced to a single point.

**[14:36]** A standard probability trick question: A person has two children. Assume each child is independently a boy or a girl with probability $1/2$. You ask, "Do you have any boys?" and the answer is yes. What is the probability the person has a girl?

**[16:31]** The sample space has four equally likely outcomes: BB, BG, GB, GG, each with prior probability $1/4$. The evidence "has a boy" eliminates GG, leaving three equally likely outcomes. Among these, two include a girl, so the probability of having a girl given at least one boy is $2/3$. Formally, $P(\text{has a girl} \mid \text{has a boy}) = \frac{P(\text{BG or GB})}{P(\text{BB, BG, or GB})} = \frac{2/4}{3/4} = 2/3$.

**[18:46]** A separate question: Now suppose you meet one of the children, and it is a boy. What is the probability the other child is a girl? The answer is $1/2$. Meeting the child fixes the gender of one child. For example, if the first child is a boy, the remaining outcomes are BB and BG, equally likely, so the probability of a girl is $1/2$.

**[19:48]** This simple trick question illustrates Bayesian reasoning without explicitly using Bayes' theorem. We start with prior beliefs (the equiprobable outcomes), obtain evidence (the answer "yes" or meeting a boy), and update our beliefs, resulting in a posterior distribution over the possibilities.

**[20:29]** A classic example of Bayesian pitfalls is the prosecutor's fallacy. A murder occurs, and blood at the crime scene is certainly the killer's. The blood type is rare, present in only 1% of the population. The defendant also has this blood type. The prosecutor argues: the probability of the defendant having this blood type if innocent is 1%, so with 99% probability, they are guilty. This reasoning is incorrect.

**[22:49]** The mistake is equating $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$ with $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$. In court, we want $P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{evidence})$, but the prosecutor starts with $P(\text{blood type} \mid \text{innocent}) = 0.01$ and then incorrectly assumes $P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{blood type}) = 1 - 0.01 = 0.99$. This confuses $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$, a common error. The correct approach uses Bayes' theorem: $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{blood type}) = \frac{P(\text{blood type} \mid \text{innocent}) \cdot P(\text{innocent})}{P(\text{blood type})}$. The prior $P(\text{innocent})$ and marginal $P(\text{blood type})$ can drastically change the result. For instance, if there are two suspects, both with the blood type, the evidence tells us nothing—the probability remains $1/2$ for each—but the prosecutor's fallacious reasoning would claim 99% guilt for both, an absurdity.

**[26:23]** The defense attorney then explains the prosecutor's mistake and offers a different calculation: there are a million people living in the city...

**[26:46]** Having this rare blood type narrows the suspect pool from a million to about 10,000. The blood type alone tells us the defendant is guilty with probability about 0.01% — very weak evidence, not proof, so the defense attorney might argue it should not be admitted at all.

**[27:19]** But that does not sound right. The defense attorney's mistake is assuming that every person with that blood type, including babies and people asleep, has an equal chance of being the killer, while ignoring why the defendant is a suspect in the first place. It is not a deep probabilistic error, just a mistaken assumption about equal prior probabilities.

**[28:25]** A famous real-world example is the O.J. Simpson trial. Simpson, a successful American football player, had a history of domestic violence, and his wife was murdered. The prosecution presented this history as evidence that he could escalate to murder. The defense countered with a statistic: only 1 in 2,500 women subjected to domestic violence are actually murdered, and therefore the history is irrelevant. Remarkably, the court agreed and instructed the jury to disregard the evidence.

**[31:06]** This is exactly the same kind of mistake. The statistic they quoted — $P(\text{murdered} \mid \text{history of domestic violence}) = 1/2500$ — is correct, but that is not what the court needed to evaluate. That statistic starts from a point where everyone is alive and measures the risk of being murdered. A murder trial argues in the opposite direction: given that a murder has already occurred and there is a history of domestic violence, what is the probability that the husband did it? If you compute the correct conditional probability from the same data, it comes out to something like 7/8. That is, among women who were murdered with a history of domestic violence, the husband was the perpetrator in the vast majority of cases. Presented correctly, the evidence would look completely different.

**[33:41]** This illustrates that people routinely confuse conditional probabilities, and it is good practice to explicitly understand exactly what you are estimating — what is on the left of the vertical bar and what is on the right — even when you cannot compute the numbers exactly. Qualitatively framing the conditional probability often makes decisions much clearer.

**[34:52]** Now we turn to our first real example: linear regression and logistic regression. These are directly relevant to neural networks. The simplest possible neural network is a linear model — one perceptron — and linear and logistic regression remain fundamental to what happens in modern networks.

**[36:04]** Linear regression finds a straight line through data points. We have a target variable $y$ and one or more input features $x$. The assumption is that $y$ depends linearly on $x$, up to noise. In general, $x$ is a vector, and we model $y$ as a weighted sum: $y = w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_d x_d$. To avoid writing the intercept $w_0$ separately, we can absorb it by adding a constant feature $x_0 = 1$, mapping the input vector $x$ to $(1, x_1, \dots, x_d)$. Then the dot product $w^T x$ automatically includes the bias term, so we can write simply $y = w^T x$.

**[39:39]** In linear regression, we want $y$ to be similar to a linear function of $x$. To formalize “near,” we minimize a measure of error between actual values and predictions. The standard choice is the sum of squared differences: $\sum_n (y_n - w x_n)^2$, minimized with respect to $w$. The model’s predictions are points on the line $w x_n$; the line is close if these vertical distances are small. This loss is symmetric, so positive and negative errors are penalized equally.

**[42:08]** But why squares? Many symmetric, monotone functions of distance—absolute value, fourth power, exponential of absolute error—would also work, and minimizing them would give different $w$. One traditional answer is the Gauss–Markov theorem: ordinary least squares gives the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE). However, the deeper reason is probabilistic.

**[44:00]** The squared loss follows from probabilistic assumptions. We cast linear regression as a machine learning model with parameter $\theta = w$, data pairs $(x_n, y_n)$, and likelihood $p(D \mid w) = \prod_n p(y_n \mid x_n, w)$, assuming conditional independence (a standard supervised learning assumption because we only model $y$ given $x$). For each $x_n$, we model $y_n$ as a random variable whose most probable value is the linear prediction $w x_n$, with a symmetric, bell‑shaped distribution decreasing away from this center. The natural default is the Gaussian, because of the central limit theorem: if the overall error arises from the sum of many small, independent perturbations (e.g., countless microscopic impacts on a rolling ball in a physics experiment), the total error tends to be Gaussian, unless there is a systematic bias. Thus we assume $y_n \mid x_n, w \sim \mathcal{N}(w x_n, \sigma^2)$. Maximizing the Gaussian likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared errors, justifying the squared loss.

**[50:39]** The normal distribution is important because of the central limit theorem: everything converges to a Gaussian. So we assume a normal distribution of $y_n$ around $w \cdot x_n$ with variance $\sigma^2$, a one-dimensional Gaussian. The second assumption is that the center is a linear function—that's linear regression—and the third is that the noise is Gaussian. There is also a fourth assumption.

**[51:41]** The Gaussian density is $p(y_n|x_n, w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(y_n - w\cdot x_n)^2\right)$. To obtain the maximum likelihood hypothesis for the weights, we maximize the likelihood, which is a product over data points. The standard trick is to take the logarithm, turning the product into a sum and avoiding numerical underflow. So $\log p(D|w) = \sum_n \left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(y_n - w\cdot x_n)^2\right] = -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n (y_n - w\cdot x_n)^2$. Since the first term is constant with respect to $w$ and there is a minus sign, maximizing this log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squares $\sum_n (y_n - w\cdot x_n)^2$. That is the motivation behind squared error—specifically squares, not absolute values or other exponents. But the story does not stop here.

**[54:51]** The fourth assumption is homoscedasticity: the noise variance $\sigma^2$ is the same across the entire dataset. This allowed us to pull $\sigma^2$ out of the sum. In practice this can be violated—for example, data may start dense and later become more noisy. If that happens, you need to modify the loss function. Understanding the probabilistic approach is crucial because it reveals the assumptions behind the sum of squares. When those assumptions break (e.g., changing variance, or noise that is only in one direction requiring an asymmetric distribution), we can notice and adapt. That is why we study the derivation, not just the final loss.

**[57:44]** Now we apply the Bayesian perspective: we want the posterior $p(w|D)$ rather than just the likelihood $p(D|w)$, so we need a prior over the weights. Unlike the coin example, here everything is continuous, and we must move from a single one-dimensional Gaussian to a multi-dimensional distribution over several weights. Maximizing the posterior adds a log prior term to the log likelihood: $\log p(w|D) \propto \log p(D|w) + \log p(w)$. The log prior is exactly what we call a regularizer in machine learning; regularization is the process of putting a prior on the model parameters.

**[1:00:17]** The regularizer depends only on the weights, not on the data—it encodes our prior beliefs about the parameters before seeing any data. For a coin, we have a strong prior that it is approximately fair. For linear regression, it may seem we have no preference among slopes; all appear equally likely a priori. Yet, to illustrate how priors actually work, consider a one‑dimensional input transformed via feature extraction. Often we do not use raw $x$ directly; we first extract features $\phi(x)$ and then learn $w \cdot \phi(x)$. This is a standard technique.

**[1:03:07]** If you want to do linear regression on images, you first need to extract semantically rich features before applying linear regression. Conversely, sometimes X is simple but you need a more complicated dependency, so you need to extract additional features. Consider one-dimensional regression with only one X and one Y, but the points clearly do not lie on a straight line — they look like a quadratic function. Can we use linear regression to learn a parabola? Yes, by transforming x into a feature vector $(1, x, x^2)$. The loss function minimizes $\sum (y_n - (w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2))^2$, so we are effectively fitting a parabola.

**[1:05:18]** This discussion is extremely relevant to deep learning. Neural networks always learn good feature extractors. Before deep learning, we would hand-craft the feature extraction and then run relatively simple models. Neural networks make feature extraction part of the model, with its own weights. In a typical architecture, the input passes through many layers that do feature extraction, producing a vector of numbers that feeds into the final layer. If the output is a number (regression), the final layer is almost always linear. So the earlier layers act as a feature extractor for the final linear regression layer, and the hard part is learning those preceding layers. A large neural network that predicts a number essentially ends with linear regression; for classification, it ends with logistic regression. That is why linear models remain very relevant for deep learning.

**[1:08:09]** Back to linear regression. I will show a polynomial regression example. The true function is a sine wave, which is not a polynomial, so we do not expect to capture it exactly. We take one period of the sine wave and place points around it. The stars are the points, the horizontal axis is x (one-dimensional), and the vertical axis is y.

**[1:09:39]** The points are clearly not linear. Fitting linear regression gives a straight line with high error. What degree of polynomial would be appropriate? A parabola (degree 2) still does not capture the shape well. A third-degree polynomial can have one minimum and one maximum, which matches one period of a sine wave. Training a third-degree polynomial (four weights, including the intercept) already gives a pretty good fit. Increasing the degree to 4 or 5 produces curves that look approximately the same, with only minor improvements.

**[1:11:50]** Will it break if we keep increasing the degree? With 11 points, a polynomial of degree 10 can pass through every point exactly — interpolation. The training error becomes zero, but the test error is huge; the curve oscillates with strange peaks and is useless for prediction. This is the classical picture of overfitting: too many parameters and it becomes obvious.

**[1:13:53]** How do we notice this overfitting when we cannot look at a picture? In this simple 1D case, with 11 points you would never use a degree-10 polynomial; it is easy to avoid. In practice, with high-dimensional data, overfitting is harder to avoid completely. In one dimension, nothing bad will ever happen. But when you have points of dimension d and you try to do linear regression, you can fail.

**[1:15:34]** If the relationship is not linear, the next step is a quadratic dependency. In one dimension, adding a quadratic term just adds one feature and you can incrementally increase the degree; in multiple dimensions it does not work that way. For a \(d\)-dimensional input, the quadratic feature vector includes \(1, x_1, \dots, x_d\), then \(x_1^2, x_1 x_2, x_1 x_3, \dots, x_d^2\). The number of features is on the order of \(d^2\). Starting from 100 features, moving to quadratics gives about 5,000 features, not 200 or 500, and this massive increase leads to overfitting.

**[1:17:49]** In high dimensions there is no middle ground—you cannot use a fractional degree. Moreover, you do not need all quadratic interactions, but you do not know in advance which ones matter. In statistics these product features are called interactions; learning which interactions are relevant is the whole point, and to do that you must start with all of them. Overfitting is therefore inherent in machine learning, even for linear regression with feature expansion, let alone for neural networks.

**[1:18:46]** How can we detect overfitting without looking at plots? The standard approach is to use a validation set: hold out some data, monitor validation error, and stop when it starts to increase. But in this high-dimensional quadratic expansion, early stopping via validation is impractical because you cannot add terms gradually; adding all at once causes overfitting, and there is no continuous stopping point.

**[1:20:29]** Another symptom is looking at the coefficients. As the polynomial degree increases, the coefficients grow but remain bounded. When overfitting begins (at degree 10 in this example), they suddenly jump to magnitudes like \(\pm 50\). Intuitively, if you have around ten points with \(x \in [-1.5, 2]\) and \(y \in [-1, 1]\), coefficients of size 50 are implausible. Formally, this reflects a prior belief that weights should be small, on the order of \(\pm 1\) or \(2\), not \(50\).

**[1:22:35]** A helpful picture: in 1D, linear regression has a 2-dimensional weight space, quadratic gives 3 dimensions, degree 4 gives even more. Higher-degree polynomials include all lower-degree ones, so the set of possible models grows. At some degree (e.g., 10) this variety becomes excessive, leading to overfitting. One way to combat this is to reduce the dimension. If you cannot reduce the feature set—as in the multidimensional quadratic case—you can instead constrain the weights within the large space, for example by restricting them to be near zero. This is what regularization and prior distributions do.

**[1:25:05]** To implement this, we introduce a prior distribution expressing the belief that weights are small, concentrated near zero but with support everywhere. A natural choice is a Gaussian centered at zero. Since we have no prior reason to distinguish weights or correlations, we use an identity covariance matrix, making the weights independent. The log prior is then a constant minus \(\frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2} \|W\|^2 = -\frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2} \sum W_i^2\). Adding this log prior to the log-likelihood yields a regularized loss function.

**[1:27:31]** The likelihood term \(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_n (y_n - W x_n)^2\) and a Gaussian prior \(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}\sum_i W_i^2\) are minimized together. After absorbing constants, this becomes \(\sum_n (y_n - W x_n)^2 + \lambda \sum_i W_i^2\), a sum of squares with an L2 regularizer. The L2 regularizer is the squared Euclidean norm (L2 norm) of the weight vector. L2 regularization can mitigate overfitting.

**[1:29:01]** Overfitting and regularization also depend on dataset size. With only 11 points, a degree‑10 polynomial severely overfits; with 100 points it would be fine. In the following L2 regularization demonstration, the unregularized fit is the interpolation polynomial. The coefficient \(\alpha\) (the regularizer strength) governs the trade‑off: as \(\alpha\) increases, the shape is increasingly determined by the regularizer rather than the data. Even a small \(\alpha\) like \(10^{-3}\) already reduces coefficient magnitudes, though some local extremes may remain. For a particular random set of points a very small regularization may be enough. Gradually increasing \(\alpha\) smooths out the overfitting; with a well‑chosen \(\alpha\) the degree‑10 polynomial still has nonzero coefficients but behaves well. If \(\alpha\) is too large (e.g., 10), the polynomial ignores the data and becomes a poor model. There is always a trade‑off.

**[1:33:04]** The L2 regularizer corresponds to a Gaussian prior, which is convenient because both the likelihood and the prior are quadratic in \(W\), giving a conjugate prior for linear regression. However, other regularizers are possible. A common alternative is L1 regularization: instead of \(\sum W_i^2\), add \(\sum |W_i|\). While the noise model (likelihood) should be chosen correctly, the prior is our choice. L1‑regularized linear regression is the LASSO, and L2‑regularized is Ridge regression. With the same regularization weight, LASSO also yields a smooth curve without overfitting, but many coefficients become exactly zero; Ridge shrinks all coefficients but keeps them nonzero. L1 is sparsity‑inducing, which is desirable for feature selection: rather than setting an arbitrary threshold, one can let the regularization drive some coefficients to zero.

**[01:39:25]** I want to highlight two important points. First, linear regression is still relevant to any complicated neural network and we will see it in architectures. Most importantly, all the probabilistic machinery — likelihoods, priors, probabilistic assumptions — and how they come together is giving us the loss functions in deep learning.

**[01:40:14]** When I write a general formula, the model appears only in one place, and the noise distribution is separate. If I keep the noise distribution Gaussian, I can change the model to any function, though optimizing may be harder and the problem will no longer be quadratic. Regardless, I still obtain the same kind of loss function — square deviation — and the same kind of regularizer if I want. This is a very common loss function regardless of the model: a neural network, a complex neural network, anything we discuss later. The model gets plugged into a loss function that comes from the probabilistic assumptions we make.

**[01:42:04]** We will briefly discuss logistic regression, focusing on the main assumption behind the softmax function, and then move to neural networks.

**[02:03:22]** Before the break we discussed linear regression: sum of squares, the Gaussian noise hypothesis. We did not derive the posterior or predictive distributions, but it is time to move on to classification, which is even more important for deep learning than linear regression and predicting numbers.

**[02:04:27]** Suppose we are solving a classification problem — distinguishing cats from dogs, finding a separating decision surface between sets of points. The difference from regression is the nature of the target variable $Y$. Classification is still supervised learning with a dataset, but $Y_N$ is now a class label, a discrete variable with a finite set of values. Since we are not predicting a number, we still want a numerical output. The problem with a discrete target variable is illustrated by a simple threshold classifier: one input, two classes, output zero below a threshold and one above. The output function is piecewise constant, so its gradient is zero everywhere. If the input is a function of $x$ with weights — a feature extractor — we cannot train it, because the gradient gives no information. If we tweak the weights slightly, the input to the threshold moves, but the output does not change until it suddenly flips, which cannot be trained through.

**[02:08:57]** For practical and conceptual reasons — including estimating uncertainty — we want the output of a classifier to be a probability distribution over the classes. With two classes $c_1$ and $c_2$, we want $P(c_1 \mid x)$, evaluating how likely a new point belongs to the first class.

**[02:09:58]** A straightforward reasoning behind logistic regression: we want a linear model with a linear separating surface, something involving $w \cdot x$. We cannot model the probability directly as a linear function of the inputs, because a probability is bounded between zero and one while a linear function is unbounded. Instead, we can use Bayes' theorem:

$$ P(c_1 \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid c_1) P(c_1)}{P(x \mid c_1) P(c_1) + P(x \mid c_2) P(c_2)} $$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $P(x \mid c_1) P(c_1)$ gives

$$ P(c_1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{P(x \mid c_2) P(c_2)}{P(x \mid c_1) P(c_1)}} $$

**[02:13:04]** The ratio inside is also $P(c_2 \mid x) / P(c_1 \mid x)$.

**[2:13:15]** Odds are the ratio of the probability of one outcome to the probability of the other outcome (e.g., 50-to-1 odds). They can take any value from $0$ to $\infty$, but because they are a ratio of probabilities they cannot be negative. A linear function would be negative over half its domain, so modeling odds directly with a linear function is not natural.

**[2:14:26]** The solution is to take the logarithm of the odds. Since odds range from $0$ to $\infty$, the log odds range from $-\infty$ to $\infty$. It is common to include a minus sign (which merely flips the fraction); the resulting quantity $\log \frac{P(c2)}{P(c1)}$ is the log odds. Because it spans the entire real line, it can be modeled as a linear function: when the log odds tend to $-\infty$ the probability goes to $0$, at $+\infty$ the probability goes to $1$, and it is monotonic in between.

**[2:15:46]** Logistic regression is exactly this model: we set $p(c1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-w \cdot x}}$, where $w \cdot x$ is a linear function of the features. The function $\sigma(a) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}$ is the logistic sigmoid; it passes through $\frac12$ at $a=0$, tends to $1$ on the right and $0$ on the left, and is a fundamental function in machine learning.

**[2:16:42]** For the second class, $p(c2 \mid x) = 1 - \sigma(w \cdot x)$. The likelihood over a dataset is therefore a product of terms that alternate between $\sigma$ and $1-\sigma$, making it more complex than the quadratic form of linear regression. There is no closed-form optimum, but it can be optimized with gradient descent or second-order methods, and priors can be added for regularization. This is still a linear model—the input enters through the linear combination $w \cdot x$—and it is a natural model for binary classification.

**[2:18:20]** To handle $K$ classes we need $K-1$ degrees of freedom. We assign each class $k$ a score $w_k \cdot x$ and model the posterior as $P(c_k \mid x) = \frac{e^{w_k \cdot x}}{\sum_{s} e^{w_s \cdot x}}$. This vector function is called the softmax. Because adding a constant to all scores does not change the output (the constant cancels after exponentiation), the scores only need to reflect relative affinities; negative values are unproblematic.

**[2:22:29]** The softmax exaggerates differences: if one score is much larger than the others, the output concentrates nearly all probability on that class. It is a soft, differentiable version of the argmax, making it suitable for gradient-based training. Almost every neural network ends with a softmax layer: after internal feature extraction, a final linear layer produces a score for each class, and the softmax converts them into a probability distribution. In a large language model, the last layer is exactly a multi-class logistic regression via the softmax, yielding the probabilities over the next token.

**[02:25:24]** Some input goes through the neural network to produce a final feature vector — semantically rich, intricate extracted features. Whether the image is a cat or a dog is already represented somewhere in these features, but we are still not sure exactly where or how to extract it. The simplest way to process these extracted features is to apply a weight matrix. We multiply $W$ times $Z$. If $Z$ has dimension $D$ and we have $K$ classes, $W$ has dimension $D \times K$, so $WZ$ becomes the vector of scores. Passing this through softmax yields a vector of class probabilities. The loss function is typically the cross-entropy between the output and the target. In classification, the target is a one-hot vector. With this target, cross-entropy simplifies to penalizing the negative log probability of the correct class — we want $- \log P(\text{correct class})$ to be low. That is essentially how classification works in deep learning. The interesting feature extraction happens earlier in the network, but at the end, you inevitably need a vector of probabilities. You cannot usually get probabilities directly; what you get is a vector of scores reflecting how well the input matches each class, and you convert them.

**[02:29:48]** Logistic regression is a linear model plus a sigmoid on top. Even for logistic regression, we cannot hope for a closed-form solution. With a quadratic function you can write down the answer — it is a system of linear equations — but once you have a sum of logs of sigmoids, there is no closed form. The only option is an iterative optimization algorithm. The main one is gradient descent. In neural networks, we must restrict ourselves to variations of gradient descent. You start somewhere and move in the direction where the loss function decreases most quickly, which is opposite to the gradient. You keep going until you find a local minimum.

**[02:33:30]** Artificial neural networks are named after natural ones, a natural motivation for scientists building thinking machines. Studying the brain provides computational insights. The brain consists of neurons, which from a computational perspective are basically all the same. A neuron has a processing center, the soma, and two kinds of tendrils. Dendrites are relatively short, attach to the outputs of other neurons, and provide inputs — thousands per neuron. The axon is the output part, and can be up to a meter long. Computationally, every neuron has many inputs and one output. Biologically, it is difficult to reliably capture exact signal amplitudes; it is much easier to capture individual events — spikes. Neurons work in spikes, not modulating signal amplitude.

**[2:38:03]** A spike is the electrical signal that a neuron sends out through its axon. Neurons always fire spikes along their axons, and other neurons pick up the signals. The spikes themselves are more or less identical; any variation is inconsequential. What varies is the frequency. A relaxed neuron might fire at 10–20 spikes per second, while an excited one can reach 200 spikes per second. Thus, the neuron conveys a numerical value: more frequent spikes mean greater excitation, not a simple binary state. Each spike is essentially one bit of information, but the intervals between them carry the signal.

**[2:39:19]** The human brain contains about 100 billion neurons, each with on average roughly 7,000 synaptic connections to other neurons. In biological brains, the weight resides in the synapse—the connection between a dendrite and an axon. These synapses can be reinforced or inhibited, giving effective positive or negative weights, and they are learned. Multiplying, the brain has on the order of a quadrillion (or slightly less) trainable weights. While artificial neural networks may have billions or even trillions of parameters, they have not yet reached the sheer synaptic count of the brain.

**[2:41:17]** However, the brain is extremely slow as a computer: neurons communicate via spikes at frequencies of only 10 to a couple hundred hertz—mere hertz, not kilohertz or megahertz. Thus, while it has an enormous number of connections, its clock speed is very low. Consequently, the brain relies on massively parallel computation and cannot afford long sequential chains. For example, recognizing a face—a difficult computer vision task—takes us just 200–300 milliseconds from stimulus to a button press. This entire cycle includes feature extraction, decision-making, and motor commands. Given the firing frequencies, the brain can perform only about 10 to 15 sequential steps in that time; it must get the answer out quickly. Therefore, computations are highly parallel but shallow. When we recognize a face, large regions of the brain activate in a few sequential waves.

**[2:44:07]** We preserve this property in artificial neural networks. While we will discuss very deep networks with hundreds of layers, in practice we cannot yet effectively use tens of thousands of layers. Current state-of-the-art models like GPT-5 are extremely wide (e.g., mixtures of experts), but in terms of sequential computational depth, they likely have only a few dozen to a couple of hundred layers. The brain's wide-but-shallow architecture is something we still imitate.

**[2:45:34]** The key motivation for neural networks is not the exact biological architecture, which is different, but the brain's remarkable versatility, adaptability, and plasticity. We can learn complex tasks from very few examples, adapt to new data sources, and even rewire functions—a phenomenon known as neuroplasticity. In a classic experiment, a small electrode plate placed on the tongue receives signals from a forehead camera; initially the tingling is ignored, but over days the brain discovers the correlation between vision and the tactile pattern. After a few weeks, people can navigate blindfolded using this new "sense." Similarly, after a stroke, undamaged brain regions can sometimes take over lost functions. These examples show that while the brain has evolutionarily predetermined areas (visual cortex in the occipital lobe, frontal cortex for reasoning), the neurons themselves are the same. There is a single learning algorithm at work, not dedicated circuits for, say, distinguishing cats from dogs or understanding 3D structure. Those abilities are not encoded in the genes—there isn't enough genetic information—but are learned from scratch with extraordinary efficiency during early life. This efficient, general learning capability is what we aim to bring to computers.

**[2:49:42]** Artificial neural networks embrace this idea under the term connectionism. Connectionism holds, philosophically and mathematically, that connecting many simple units yields a complex system capable of interesting and powerful computations.

**[2:50:17]** It’s as simple as it gets and it’s true: there are many mathematical results showing you can approximate anything with a polynomial or with two layers of univariate functions. Neural networks are based on this idea—take very simple things, combine them, and you get something very complicated like a face recognition system. Artificial neural networks are also similar to the human brain in the structure of a single neuron.

**[2:50:49]** An artificial neuron has inputs \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\), just like the linear models we discussed. You multiply them by the weights to get a linear combination \(w \cdot x\); the weights are trained during learning. Before the output, you need a nonlinear activation function. If neurons were only linear, a network would be pointless because a composition of linear functions is still linear—the whole network would collapse to a single linear neuron. As soon as you have any nonlinearity—even something as simple as ReLU, which is just two linear functions joined together—it starts to work. There is a large body of mathematics showing that any nonlinearity is enough to approximate anything. In practice, what matters is that compositions of these simple neurons can approximate whatever you want. The question then is how to find the weights and train the network.

**[2:53:42]** This kind of model for a single artificial neuron has been with us since 1943, before the Turing test, the Dartmouth seminar, or even the term “artificial intelligence.” In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts wrote “A Logical Calculus of Nervous Activity” from the perspective of mathematical logic, aiming to create a mathematical model of the human brain, and it was basically this neuron. Modern architectures include parts like attention mechanisms in transformers that are not exactly these neurons, but most neural networks are still built from them. The model was first implemented in practice by Frank Rosenblatt in 1958 with the perceptron; it indeed worked and learned to recognize letters (simplified—specific font, fixed viewpoint, far from real OCR, but amazing for its time). The New York Times essentially declared the hard part done, predicting that computers would soon walk, talk, see, write, reproduce, and be conscious. This early optimism was unwarranted, but we still inherit a lot from these early ideas.

**[2:57:18]** Then the AI winter arrived, and people became convinced that machine learning, and neural networks in particular, did not work. Besides practical reasons, there was a strange theoretical argument that convinced many people neural networks were worthless. Whatever the activation function \(h\), the input still reaches \(h\) through a linear combination. For classification, you draw a linear separating surface: if \(h(w \cdot x) > 0\) assign class 1, else class 2, the decision boundary is \(w \cdot x = 0\), a linear surface. The nonlinearity itself does not create a nonlinear boundary; it only enables building networks. A single perceptron therefore cannot solve problems like XOR, where the orange and blue classes are linearly inseparable. People mistakenly took this as an argument against perceptrons and neural networks as a whole.

**[3:00:19]** The correct answer is that you cannot solve a linearly inseparable task with a single perceptron, but you can with a network of perceptrons. The idea of taking neurons, putting them together in layers, and computing a composition of several such neurons is also very old and goes back to Alan Turing. In 1948, before the Turing test, he wrote a paper suggesting truly deep neural networks—compositions of layers of neurons. The question was how to train them. The answer is gradient descent: you need to take derivatives through the computational graph, which is done by backpropagation—simply the chain rule for computing derivatives. Backpropagation was independently discovered many times and was already well understood by the 1970s as a way to update weights through layers of simple functions. By the late 1970s and first half of the 1980s, the idea of deep neural networks existed and had started to appear. By the second half of the 1980s, you already had convolutional networks and recurrent networks; transformers came later. **[3:03:26]** If you take a neural network architecture from the early 1990s or late 1980s, mechanically scale it up, and train it on modern hardware and datasets, it works pretty well—of course worse than today’s networks, but such results back in 1990 would have been state-of-the-art and never discouraged researchers. Unfortunately, neural networks require a certain scale: with a very small network and dataset, a specialized model built by thinking about the problem often outperforms. By the early 1990s, lacking the compute and data to scale up, the research community reached the consensus that neural networks are the second-best way of doing just about anything—universal but always worse than a domain-specific model. Consequently, neural networks were largely forgotten in the 1990s and early 2000s; it was the heyday of probabilistic and graphical models (interesting and important, but out of scope for this course).

**[3:05:14]** Only a few groups kept pushing: Yann LeCun working on image processing with CNNs, and the groups of Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio. They continued believing in neural networks and were finally proved right.

**[3:05:36]** The deep learning revolution began around 2006–2007. The key technical innovation—still relevant—was moving computation from CPUs to GPUs. Neural network operations are massively parallelizable, much like the brain, but only in the early 2000s did widely available gaming GPUs make such parallelism practical. Hinton’s group moved computations to GPUs, gaining roughly a 10×–20× efficiency boost, enough to tip neural networks from not working to working.

**[3:07:06]** Starting in the late 2000s, neural networks began revolutionizing one field of machine learning after another. The first was speech processing: around 2010, deep neural networks served as excellent feature extractors, making Siri (2011) possible; classical speech recognition would not have worked well enough. Image processing and computer vision were revolutionized around 2011–2012 with AlexNet (which we will study in detail). Then recurrent networks brought deep learning into natural language processing; by 2014–2015, neural machine translation was common. In 2017, everything changed again with the transformer, which transformed the industry and defines the present era.

**[3:09:23]** For classification, a neural network needs to output a probability vector. After the main part of the network extracts features (a $d$-dimensional vector or tensor), we must convert these features into $k$ class scores that sum to one. A linear transformation followed by softmax achieves this, effectively making the last layer logistic regression on the extracted features—not on the raw input. If the task were regression, the same structure would apply but with a linear output (and typically $k=1$) without softmax.

**[3:13:26]** Regularization differs from model selection. In regularization, the model structure and number of parameters are fixed—you penalize parameter values (e.g., $L_2$, $L_1$) but do not change the model form. Model selection criteria like adjusted $R^2$, Akaike information criterion (AIC), or Bayesian information criterion (BIC) are used when you have several candidate models (e.g., linear, quadratic, cubic regressions) and need to choose among them by trading off goodness-of-fit and complexity. The polynomial example earlier muddled this distinction because changing the degree changed the number of parameters, but regularization proper only influences the values of the parameters, not how many there are.

**[3:15:30]** It is deceptively simple. I want to estimate the likelihood, the probability of having model $k$ for my dataset. I get this probability from Bayes' rule: $p(m_k) \, p(d \mid m_k)$. Here $m$ is the whole model, not a specific set of weights—for example, linear regression versus quadratic regression. Bayes' rule still applies: without prior preference, assume a uniform prior and just compare $p(d \mid m_k)$. Where does that $p(d \mid m_k)$ appear? Going back to Bayes' rule at the parameter level, $p(\theta \mid d) = \frac{p(\theta) \, p(d \mid \theta)}{p(d)}$, you can add the model index without changing the formula: $p(\theta \mid d, m_k) = \frac{p(\theta \mid m_k) \, p(d \mid \theta, m_k)}{p(d \mid m_k)}$. The denominator in Bayes' rule, the normalizing constant $p(d \mid m_k)$, is exactly the quantity you compare when choosing between models. All information criteria are basically approximations to compute this evidence. So model selection is about trying to estimate this normalizing constant and choosing the model with the largest one. There are two levels: selecting the model, and then inside the model, actually learning the parameters.
