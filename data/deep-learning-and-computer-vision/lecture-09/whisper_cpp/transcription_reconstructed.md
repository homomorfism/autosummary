But generative models are important and are a great teaching tool, demonstrating ideas that go through the whole of machine learning. Today we talk about variational autoencoders, where variational approximation appears in its simplest and already useful form. **[00:02:37]** Recall the autoencoder: an input $X$ (e.g., a picture) passes through an encoder to produce a latent code $Z$, which is smaller than $X$. A decoder then attempts to reconstruct the original $X$, producing $\hat{X}$. Training is straightforward: minimize a reconstruction loss, such as $L_2$ norm or perceptual loss, that measures similarity. Autoencoders require no labels; the dataset provides its own targets since you reconstruct the same $X$.

**[00:04:41]** The big question is why we cannot use the decoder as a generative model. The latent codes live on a structured manifold within $Z$, but they do not cover the entire space. If you sample a random point in $Z$, it will not fall on the manifold of plausible latent codes, so the decoder won’t produce a good image. One approach is the adversarial autoencoder: add a discriminator that distinguishes real $Z$ sampled from a target distribution (e.g., a Gaussian) from the encoder’s outputs, applying the GAN idea to regularize the latent space.

**[00:07:00]** Today we discuss a different approach: the variational autoencoder. The core idea is to make the encoder output a whole distribution $p(z \mid x)$ rather than a single point. For a given cat picture, the encoder outputs, say, a Gaussian distribution in latent space. You then sample a point from that distribution and reconstruct the same cat. By doing this for all training points, the distributions can overlap and cover the latent space, making it amenable to random sampling.

**[00:12:42]** In practice, the encoder outputs mean $\mu(x)$ and standard deviation $\sigma(x)$ for a diagonal Gaussian. You construct the Gaussian from these parameters, sample $Z$, and pass it to the decoder. However, the encoder naturally prefers to cheat: it can shrink the variance to zero, effectively collapsing to a single point, or push the means far apart to avoid overlap. Both would defeat the purpose. Therefore, we need a regularization term on $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to prevent $\sigma$ from collapsing to zero and $\mu$ from diverging.

**[16:24]** The regularization is very simple: we want the latent distribution to be similar to a standard normal distribution with mean zero and unit covariance matrix. This is the idea of the Variational Autoencoder, but two problems arise. The first is how to formalize this; we know what we want—a reconstruction loss that makes $\hat{x}$ similar to $x$, and a regularizer that keeps $\mu$ close to zero and $\sigma$ not too close to zero—but there are many ways to measure closeness between distributions. Which reconstruction loss should we choose? The second problem, specific to this architecture, is how to insert a sampling procedure into a deep neural network. In the forward pass, sampling from the Gaussian is straightforward, but gradients cannot flow through a sampling operation, so the backward pass breaks. We need to solve this.

**[19:48]** To see where the loss functions come from, let us take a more formal approach. Consider the two variables in our model: $x$ (the image) and $z$ (the latent code). Every machine learning model takes the joint distribution $p(x,z)$ and decomposes it. There are two natural ways: $p(x,z) = p(x) \, p(z \mid x)$ or $p(x,z) = p(z) \, p(x \mid z)$. These correspond exactly to the neural network components. $p(z \mid x)$ is the encoder distribution—the distribution the encoder produces. $p(x \mid z)$ is the decoder distribution; the decoder takes $z$ and outputs an approximation $\hat{x}$, but in reality, just as in linear regression, it produces a noise distribution around $x$, typically a Gaussian. $p(x)$ is the true, highly complex global distribution of the data (e.g., cat pictures), which we cannot comprehend directly. $p(z)$ is the prior distribution on latent codes, which we need to be a simple distribution we can sample from to generate new data: we sample $z \sim p(z)$ and then $x \sim p(x \mid z)$.

**[23:56]** If we want $p(z)$ to be simple, say a standard Gaussian, and we also assume the decoder noise is Gaussian, then the product $p(z) \, p(x \mid z)$ is Gaussian. But $p(x)$ is extremely complicated; cat pictures cannot be Gaussian. Therefore, we cannot assume both the encoder and the decoder are simple. The more natural choice is to keep the decoder simple (Gaussian output noise), which forces the true encoder distribution $p(z \mid x)$ to be very complicated. In a Variational Autoencoder, we acknowledge this and approximate the complicated $p(z \mid x)$ with a simple distribution $q(z \mid x)$, specifically a Gaussian whose parameters are output by the encoder network. This is a variational approximation, one of the most fundamental ideas in machine learning. So we are motivated to find how to construct this approximation.

**[30:05]** Starting from the identity $\log P(X,Z)$, we write $\log P(X) = \log P(X,Z) - \log P(Z|X)$. Taking the expectation with respect to an arbitrary distribution $Q(Z)$ gives $\log P(X) = \mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}[\log P(X,Z) - \log P(Z|X)]$, since $\log P(X)$ is constant in $Z$. We then add and subtract the entropy term $\mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}[\log Q(Z)]$, which does not change the equality, and rearrange: $\log P(X) = \mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}\!\left[\log \frac{P(X,Z)}{Q(Z)}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}\!\left[\log \frac{Q(Z)}{P(Z|X)}\right]$.

**[34:32]** The second term is exactly the Kullback–Leibler divergence $KL(Q(Z) \,\|\, P(Z|X))$, which measures how close $Q(Z)$ is to the true posterior. We therefore have $\log P(X) = \mathcal{L}(Q) + KL(Q(Z) \,\|\, P(Z|X))$, where $\mathcal{L}(Q)$ is the first expectation. Because $\log P(X)$ is a constant with respect to $Q$, minimizing the KL divergence is equivalent to maximizing $\mathcal{L}(Q)$. This $\mathcal{L}(Q)$ is called the variational lower bound, or evidence lower bound (ELBO).

**[38:09]** The key practical gain is that $\mathcal{L}(Q)$ involves only $P(X,Z)$, the joint distribution of observed and hidden variables, rather than the intractable posterior $P(Z|X)$. In typical probabilistic models—including the variational autoencoder—the joint distribution is simple (for example, a product of Gaussian distributions), making optimization feasible. The derivation effectively replaces a complicated, unworkable distribution with a simple one.

**[43:04]** The variational approximation is used in practice by going through the math: you substitute your specific distributions and model, which yields the loss functions. For the variational autoencoder, we continue from the equality. Plugging in the decomposition, we get $\mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}[\log P(X \mid Z)] + \mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}[\log P(Z)] - \mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}[\log Q(Z)]$. Combining the second and third terms gives $-\mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}\!\left[\log\frac{Q(Z)}{P(Z)}\right]$, which is the KL divergence between $Q(Z)$ and $P(Z)$. Here $Q(Z)$ is our approximate distribution, a Gaussian with parameters $\mu_X$ and $\sigma_X$ output by the encoder, and $P(Z)$ is the simple prior distribution we want to sample from—a standard Gaussian. So this term says that our Gaussian $Q$ should be close to a standard Gaussian; this is exactly the regularizer.

**[46:02]** This is consistent with our earlier intuition but is now precise. We anticipated needing a regularizer to prevent the latent distributions from floating apart and collapsing to zero variance, but we had no principled form. The variational approximation gives an exact formula: a specific function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ that you can implement in PyTorch. You would not be able to guess this formula; the KL divergence emerges necessarily. Any other arbitrary regularizer (e.g., placing a gamma prior on $\sigma$) would be just one of many heuristics, whereas here we see the principled reason for this choice.

**[47:56]** The variational approximation therefore yields the loss functions. The first term, $\mathbb{E}_{Q(Z)}[\log P(X \mid Z)]$, involves the decoder distribution. We model $P(X \mid Z)$ as a Gaussian with mean given by the decoder output and some noise variance. Expanding the log-density (ignoring constants) gives a term proportional to $-\|X - \text{decoder}(Z)\|^2$. Taking the expectation over $Q(Z)$ yields the reconstruction loss. In practice, we approximate the expectation with the empirical average over the training set, so the reconstruction loss becomes $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \text{decoder}(z_i)\|^2$, where $z_i$ is obtained from the encoder output for $x_i$.

**[51:11]** To summarize: starting from a basic decomposition and applying the variational approximation, we obtained the variational lower bound $L = -\text{const} - c \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} - \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$ is the reconstruction loss (mean squared error) and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$ is the KL regularizer. Maximizing $L$ means minimizing the sum of these two losses with respect to the encoder parameters that output $\mu$ and $\sigma$. So we have gone from the vague idea of sampling inside an autoencoder with some regularizer to exact, non-trivial formulas. That resolves the first problem. The second problem is putting gradients through the sampling step, which is solved by the reparameterization trick.

**[54:33]** The reparameterization trick works by restructuring the network. The usual VAE pipeline is: $x$ goes to the encoder, which produces $\mu$ and $\sigma$; then we need to sample $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$; and $z$ goes to the decoder to produce $\hat{x}$. To enable gradient flow through the sampling, we reparameterize the Gaussian sampling. Sampling from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is equivalent to sampling $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and then setting $z = \mu + \sigma \odot u$ (component-wise multiplication). Now we sample $u$ once as an additional input, independent of the encoder, and the transformation is deterministic. Gradients can then flow from the loss back through $z$ to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ without issue. That is the reparameterization trick; it works for Gaussians (and some other distributions) but not for all.

**[1:00:00]** Variational autoencoders: this is the motivation. It is relatively easy to train an autoencoder, but the high-dimensional data manifold is very complex, and even the lower-dimensional latent space is non-trivial. Even in a low-dimensional space, sampling from a Gaussian will not land on this manifold.

**[1:00:32]** The idea of the variational autoencoder is to have the encoder output entire distributions. For each input, we place a distribution in the latent space; we sample from it, and we must reconstruct the original cat picture from any point in that distribution. Of course, it will not always succeed, but that is the goal.

**[1:01:02]** Intuition: we want these distributions to cover the latent space. They should be wide enough and close together so that when we later sample from a standard Gaussian, any point falls in a good region, covered by one of these small distributions. Without regularization, however, the points will fly away and the variances will shrink to zero.

**[1:01:59]** Thus we need both a reconstruction loss and some regularizer for the encoder output. The two questions are: how to choose the loss function and regularizer, and how to pass gradients through the sampling process.

**[1:02:23]** We started with the equality linking encoder, decoder, and the desired $z$-distribution; this joint is extremely complicated, so both encoder and decoder cannot be simple. Instead, we model the decoder distribution as a simple round Gaussian and approximate the complicated encoder distribution also with a Gaussian. Applying the variational approximation, the variational lower bound plus the KL divergence between our approximation and the true posterior equals a constant. As we maximize the lower bound, the KL divergence shrinks. This yields the exact reconstruction loss from the decoder model and the exact regularizer to use. To pass gradients, we use the reparameterization trick: for Gaussians, simply sample from a standard Gaussian, multiply by $\sigma$, add $\mu$, and feed to the decoder. That is the proper way to train autoencoders.

**[1:04:08]** When VAEs first appeared, they did not catch on. Early papers from ten years ago used them as a baseline that was not difficult to beat. Since then, VAEs have been extended; for example, to discrete latent spaces, where $Z$ is a sequence of tokens. This opened up possibilities like writing latent codes with a transformer from text, treating it as machine translation. That is how DALL-E worked in 2020/2021. By now, variational autoencoders underlie a lot of modern generative models. For instance, diffusion models usually operate not in pixel space but in a latent space. It is too computationally expensive to diffuse on pixels. So they operate in the latent space of a modern variational autoencoder, such as VQ-VAE, which has a discrete latent space and adds adversarial losses to ensure local patch realism. Modern diffusion models often work in the latent space of such models.

**[1:08:13]** Now, diffusion models. This is our last big idea. The idea is simple in essence but mathematically very detailed. Like VAEs, it uses a variational bound, but much more difficult. I will just give the basic idea. The foundational paper is from 2015 by Sohl-Dickstein et al., "Deep Unsupervised Learning Using Non-Equilibrium Thermodynamics." The physics analogy here comes from a basic fact about Markov chains, not statistical physics. The basic idea of Markov chains is that you can train a Markov chain...

**[1:10:10]** The forward chain adds noise, gradually more and more, to the input. After enough steps—hundreds of steps—the result becomes indistinguishable from randomness. Starting with a crisp picture and adding Gaussian noise repeatedly, after about 500 steps you see just Gaussian noise indistinguishable from random sampling. The point of diffusion models is to reverse this process: turn the noising chain into a denoising chain. In the forward direction the transformation is trivial—just add Gaussian noise—but the reverse direction is a denoising procedure that removes Gaussian noise, which is much harder. You cannot do it exactly unless you know the original image. Conditional on $x_0$ (the original image) you can reverse it exactly, but we want to use it as a generative model: after training, start from random noise and gradually turn it into something real. It turns out you can approximate the reverse chain with a Gaussian, even though the true reverse distribution is complicated.

**[1:12:40]** To formalize, we write down the forward diffusion chain and derive closed-form formulas for moving several steps ahead. Then we reverse the process, deriving formulas for the reverse conditional on $x_0$ (where exact denoising is possible). Then we make a large variational approximation that jointly approximates every step in the long chain. The transformations are parametric, modeled by neural networks, with parameters $\theta$. At the start of the chain, $p(X_n)$ is a simple standard Gaussian; after the denoising chain we obtain a sample from the target distribution (e.g., cat pictures). We approximate by minimizing a loss function that is a sum of terms like Kullback-Leibler divergences between Gaussians. This loss is optimized by gradient descent to train the parameters of the reverse transition distributions.

**[1:15:53]** This is your generative model. The original diffusion model paper only worked for very small dimensions (e.g., CIFAR) and couldn't scale up. For a while it was forgotten, considered interesting but impractical because the reverse chain requires hundreds of steps—hundreds of neural network evaluations to produce one image. In 2020, denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) solved some technical problems and achieved generation quality on par with the best GANs of the time, particularly for faces, though still poor for diverse datasets like cats. DDPMs did not solve the fundamental speed problem: they still required hundreds of steps and were extremely slow.

**[1:19:27]** Later that same year, a different group solved this problem. The key idea is to switch from approximating the step from $x_{t+1}$ to $x_t$ to instead always approximating the direction to the original $x_0$. In DDIM, instead of trying to approximate the direction to the next point, you approximate the direction to the original $x_0$. In DDPM, you go step by step, requiring every intermediate point. In DDIM, on every step of the Markov chain you approximate the direction straight to the goal. While you cannot jump to $x_0$ in one step because the approximation isn't perfect, you can take much wider steps. With DDIM, you can skip steps: each step aims toward $x_0$, so even with a chain originally having hundreds of steps, you can move toward $x_0$ faster by taking longer strides.

**[1:22:11]** DDIMs allowed a 10–100× speedup over original diffusion models with no loss of quality, making diffusion models practical, though still slow and expensive.

**[1:23:23]** The final step to the modern form was performing diffusion not in pixel space but in the latent space of an autoencoder. The famous Stable Diffusion paper (2022) introduced this approach. The model uses a diffusion process and a denoising architecture that operates on latent codes. Starting from random noise $z_T$, a conditioning signal—such as an encoded text prompt—is fed into each denoising step via self-attention or similar blocks. After many denoising steps, the output latent code is decoded into an image by a variational autoencoder.

**[1:26:41]** DALL-E 2 used a similar latent diffusion idea but with pretrained CLIP embeddings, which provide a joint latent space for text and images. A decoder was trained to reconstruct images from CLIP representations, and a diffusion model was trained inside this latent space to map text representations to image representations. The generated images were impressive for the time.

**[1:28:37]** The Diffusion Transformer (DiT) later replaced the internal denoising unit with a full transformer, keeping the same overall latent diffusion framework. Flux, a direct successor of Stable Diffusion from the same team, further improved image quality and was a widely used open model, though it struggled with text rendering and complex prompt following. Current state-of-the-art text-to-image models (e.g., GPT, Gemini, Midjourney) are closed, so their exact architectures are unknown, but they are almost certainly latent diffusion models built on large autoencoders.

**[1:31:55]** To conclude, generative models can be organized into explicit and implicit density models. Among implicit models, GANs remain relevant in niche applications. Explicit density models include autoregressive models (e.g., WaveNet, LLMs that generate tokens one by one), flow-based models that gradually transform a simple distribution, and approximate density models. The latter category contains Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and diffusion models. Both avoid strong assumptions about the true data distribution and instead make assumptions about an approximation to the reverse process, optimizing via a variational bound. VAEs approximate $p(z \mid x)$; diffusion models approximate the reverse denoising chain. All major generative models in the news fall into these categories—mostly autoregressive models or VAEs/diffusion models.
