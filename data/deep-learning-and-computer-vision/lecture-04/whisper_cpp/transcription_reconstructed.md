**[00:22]** We stopped last time at stochastic gradient descent algorithms. A neural network is a large computational graph built from elementary operations. It receives two kinds of inputs: the actual input (e.g., an image) and the weights. The graph's output is a loss function $L(x, y, w)$, with the correct answer $y$ provided at the end. The overall loss $L(w)$ is the average of per-example losses over a mini-batch. We then update the weights by gradient descent: $w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla_w L$. In practice, we use more sophisticated optimizers such as Adam. That is deep learning in a nutshell.

**[03:57]** Today we turn from the loss function as a black box to what happens inside: the architecture of neural networks. An architecture is the structure of the computational graph. The graph must be a directed acyclic graph (no cycles) so that we can traverse it topologically forward and backward. Each node must implement forward and backward propagation computations. A common organization is a layered architecture. A layer is a set of neurons (perceptrons) that mostly connect to neurons of the next layer; the concept is informal and admits skip connections that bypass layers, such as residual connections, which we will discuss later. The simplest class is feed-forward fully connected networks. In a fully connected layer, neurons do not connect to each other inside the layer; they receive inputs from all neurons of the previous layer and send outputs to all neurons of the next layer. This forms a complete bipartite graph between successive layers. The final layers typically reduce to a smaller set of neurons to produce the output. Fully connected layers remain important and appear in transformers and other architectures.

**[09:35]** Today we begin discussing a more interesting architecture: convolutional neural networks. What is the basic idea? This is one of the last times we will trace the biological motivation. We discussed how artificial neural networks were inspired by the brain and connectionism. A short remark: the human brain cannot perform gradient descent. A biological neuron has many dendrites and a single axon; it can compute only one function (the forward pass). Gradient descent requires each node to compute at least two functions: forward propagation and backward propagation to compute gradients. Since neurons cannot run a backward pass, the brain must use a different learning mechanism. Moreover, that mechanism must somehow send a signal back from the ultimate goal (the loss) to every neuron, implying that the brain must have loops to propagate error signals.

**[14:26]** The biological brain requires extensive feedback connections for training; the brain does have feedback, and here the analogy with artificial networks ends. A single neuron or perceptron is a good model of a biological neuron, especially with ReLU activations, and the brain uses connectionism—composing many simple functions to produce complex behavior—but it definitely does not use gradient descent, and its architecture is fundamentally different. The brain has layers, but between every two layers there are feedback connections that go back, and from later layers there are connections that go all the way back; it is a mess. We don't understand the brain's fine architecture in detail. We know general regions like the visual cortex and its gross layers, but the fine structure is extremely difficult to ascertain.

**[16:04]** Hubel and Wiesel won the Nobel Prize for their studies of the visual cortex. Convolutional networks are partly motivated by the visual cortex, but the analogy eventually ends. In the 1960s and 1970s, they made their famous studies; their key advantage was a new device: tiny electrodes that could be attached to a single neuron in a live brain, allowing them to read off its activation. Before that, researchers could only infer brain function from stroke patients' deficits. Hubel and Wiesel could take a cat, slice open its head (under painkillers), and attach electrodes to individual neurons to see what each neuron responds to.

**[18:19]** They found that the visual cortex, located in the occipital lobe, receives visual input via the optic nerve from the eyes, and the first layer of neurons takes immediate input from the eyes. These neurons have small receptive fields—each looks at a small part of the entire picture—and recognize something very simple. For instance, a neuron might fire strongly for a bright vertical line in its field; if the line is rotated, its activation drops to near zero.

**[20:52]** Naturally, a second layer would combine these simple shapes into more complex ones. A neuron detecting vertical lines and another detecting horizontal lines could feed into a neuron that detects a corner. Hubel and Wiesel studied the second layer and found such combination-detecting neurons. However, they did not go further, which was wise because the brain's structure becomes extremely messy beyond that. While there are layers V1, V2, V4, V5, etc., it is not a clean feedforward network: there are extensive feedback connections and many interconnections, making it far more complicated than an artificial neural network. Even a diagram labeled "the most important neural pathways in the early layers of the visual cortex" shows a clear first and second layer, but beyond that a tangled mess of interconnected components.

**[23:26]** The observation that simple geometric shapes combine hierarchically, eventually enabling tasks like face recognition, inspired attempts to imitate this in computers. Convolutional networks are an old idea, dating back at least to the neocognitron by Kunihiko Fukushima in the late 1970s. In a short 1979 paper, he described a deep neural network with several layers, convolution operations, and ReLU-like hinge activations—yet he did not have a learning algorithm; the filters were hand-crafted, not trainable. The modern trainable convolutional network was developed by Yann LeCun's group, starting in the late 1980s, and is his main claim to fame.

**[25:47]** They already appeared in their modern form—they had training, convolutions, and everything.

**[25:59]** Let me walk you through a few images to show how convolutional networks work. It is a brilliant idea of what I would call structural regularization.

**[26:24]** First, consider a picture like this one. I want to process it—for example, find the little boy’s head (object detection, which we’ll discuss later).

**[26:53]** The main assumption behind CNNs is that if you are looking for some feature in an image, it is the exact same function applied everywhere. If I have a black box that looks at a small window and says “that’s a boy’s head” or “no,” I apply that same box across the whole picture; the output changes (yes here, no there), but the function is identical. The inputs differ, so the outputs differ.

**[27:53]** The idea of CNNs: break the input (a picture is easiest to reason about) into small, typically overlapping windows. For every tile, apply a small neural network. This network is trained via gradient descent, has weights, but is small—it is applied only to that little tile of a few pixels. The big gain is that you take the exact same small network and apply it to every single tile across the input. Each tile yields an output vector; usually you extract several features, so the output is multiple numbers. You then move to the next tile and repeat.

**[29:23]** Back to the board: the input picture is broken into windows. Each window passes through the small network, producing a vector of features. The next window produces a different vector, and these vectors inherit the geometric ordering—top-right window gives a top-right vector, bottom-left gives bottom-left, and so on. The output is a three-dimensional tensor, retaining width and height, and now gaining a meaningful depth. (Inputs also have depth—for example, a color image has depth three.) So a convolutional layer maps one 3D tensor to another. You can then repeat: tile the resulting tensor’s spatial dimensions with small windows, pass each small subtensor through another small network, and obtain another feature tensor. Always you work with three-dimensional arrays—confusingly called tensors in deep learning—converting one 3D tensor to another, and you can stack a whole sequence of such layers.

**[33:25]** Classical CNNs and many architectures today also use another type of layer: the pooling layer. Let me motivate it. Look at the neurons. The original image has many pixels—large width and height. We slice it into very small tiles, say 3×3 pixels (times depth for color). The resulting feature vector depends only on that 3×3 tile; we say the feature has a receptive field of 3×3—it sees only a very local part of the image. Now suppose we do another 3×3 convolution on the output tensor. A feature at this next layer looks at a 3×3 region of the previous features, each of which in turn sees a 3×3 patch of the original. So its receptive field is 5×5 in the original pixels. Each 3×3 layer adds two to the width and height of the receptive field (not multiplies). To get neurons that look at the whole 256×256 image, you would need around 130 layers—clearly impractical. This is the motivation for pooling layers.

**[38:00]** To avoid building excessively deep networks, you need to reduce the spatial geometry faster than simple stacking of convolutions. The original CNNs addressed this with pooling layers.

**[38:36]** A pooling layer takes the feature map from a convolutional layer, slices it into non-overlapping windows (typically $2 \times 2$), and in each window replaces the four values with a single one according to some rule. The two commonly used rules are max pooling (take the maximum) and average pooling (take the average); in practice, max pooling is far more common. The reason is that max pooling pushes convolutional layers to extract more meaningful features. For example, if you want a feature detector that answers “is there a little boy’s head in this tile?”, it outputs $1$ where the head is and $0$ elsewhere. When you pool over a larger region, the max gives $1$ if the head appears anywhere, whereas the average would be diluted by all the zeros. Features that detect the presence of an object obey a “max” composition, while features like average colour are better suited to averaging. Because conv nets primarily care about detecting shapes and objects, max pooling is the typical choice.

**[42:23]** When you alternate convolution and pooling layers, you can reduce geometry very rapidly. Assuming $2 \times 2$ pooling (which is standard), each pooling layer halves the width and halves the height, thus reducing the total number of values by a factor of four. After pooling you can apply another convolution, and so on.

**[43:04]** Now let us define exactly what a convolutional layer does. The word “convolution” simply means that you tile the input with small windows and apply the same transformation to each window independently. The transformation itself need not be anything specific, but when people say “convolutional layer” they usually mean the simplest neural network possible: a single fully connected layer applied to each tile.

**[44:37]** Consider a three-dimensional input of size $W \times H \times D$. You cut out a tile of size $w \times h \times D$ (always taking all feature channels). This tile is a $D \times w \times h$ tensor. Inside the convolutional layer, you flatten it to a vector of length $D \cdot w \cdot h$ and multiply it by a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ of shape $(D w h) \times d'$, producing an output vector of dimension $d'$. The crucial property of a convolutional layer is weight sharing: you reuse exactly the same matrix $\mathbf{W}$ for every tile of the input. Therefore, the number of parameters is $D w h d'$, which depends only on the tile size, not on the full input dimensions. Even if you double the input image size, the layer still uses the same weights; you only need to run the convolution across the larger image.

**[48:24]** In practice, the weight matrix is not stored as a huge 2D matrix; it is far more convenient to use a four‑dimensional tensor of shape $D \times w \times h \times d'$. In PyTorch, for instance, the convolutional layer’s weight tensor has exactly these dimensions. The input is a 3D tensor matching the first three dimensions of the weight tensor, and the output is a feature map with depth $d'$.

**[49:50]** This operation is absolutely not limited to 2D images. You can define convolutional layers for any kind of input. For example, text is a sequence of tokens, each represented by a vector, giving a 2D input of shape $L \times D$ (length $\times$ depth). A 1D convolution slides a window of length $l$ over the sequence, extracting a tile of size $l \times D$, flattening it, and multiplying by a shared weight matrix. Everything else remains the same.

**[51:14]** For a sequence, the weight tensor is three-dimensional $d \times l \times d'$ and the result is a vector of size $d'$. In the other direction, a video is a sequence of images, so the input is $d \times w \times h \times t$ and you multiply by a five-dimensional weight tensor $t \times w \times h \times d \times d'$. The same idea of cutting the input into small tiles and processing each tile separately applies to any dimension; it was especially productive for images, and all examples in this class will be images.

**[52:45]** Let's work through a concrete example—a classical LeNet architecture from around 2001. It is very small, not very powerful, but we can go layer by layer to count weights and see how convolutions save a tremendous number of weights.

**[53:35]** The input is a $32 \times 32$ grayscale image, so depth one. The input tensor is $1 \times 32 \times 32$.

**[54:05]** After the first convolutional layer we have a $28 \times 28$ tensor with six feature maps (feature maps, channels, and depth all mean the same thing). So the tensor is $6 \times 28 \times 28$. How did we go from $32 \times 32$ to $28 \times 28$? With no padding, when you cover the input by overlapping tiles, the border pixels cannot be the center of a full tile. A $3 \times 3$ filter would cut off one pixel on each side, giving $30 \times 30$; here we lose four pixels total, so the filter must be $5 \times 5$.

**[59:22]** The weight tensor for this layer is $1 \times 5 \times 5 \times 6$ (input depth 1, $5 \times 5$ filter, 6 output features). That gives $5 \times 5 \times 6 = 150$ trainable weights (ignoring biases). Compare a fully connected layer from the $32 \times 32$ input to a $6 \times 28 \times 28$ output: the weight matrix would be of size $(32\cdot 32) \times (6\cdot 28\cdot 28) = 1024 \times 4704$, about 4.8 million weights. The convolutional idea reduces this to 150—a huge saving. Convolutions act as an excellent structural regularizer: they impose the constraint that exactly the same weights are applied to every image window, heavily compressing the hypothesis space.

**[01:04:10]** After the first convolutional layer comes a pooling (subsampling) layer with $2 \times 2$ windows, reducing $28 \times 28 \times 6$ to $14 \times 14 \times 6$. How many trainable weights does a pooling layer have? None—max pooling simply takes the maximum of each $2 \times 2$ block, with no learned parameters.

**[1:05:49]** Max pooling takes a $2 \times 2$ window and keeps only the maximum. It has no trainable weights—it is a deterministic function. Pooling layers have no weights and nothing to learn; you just apply the fixed operation.

**[1:07:11]** Next, a pooling layer outputs $6 \times 14 \times 14$ (no weights), followed by another convolutional layer that goes to $16 \times 10 \times 10$. This uses a $5 \times 5$ convolution that maps 6 input features to 16 output features, so the weight count is $6 \times 5 \times 5 \times 16 = 2400$ weights.

**[1:08:59]** After another pooling we get $16 \times 5 \times 5$. Now the spatial dimensions are only $5 \times 5$, so the whole tensor is a single tile—you can call it a convolutional or fully connected layer. You take the whole input and produce 120 features, then 84 features, then the 10-class output (maybe with a softmax). The first fully connected layer has $16 \times 5 \times 5 \times 120 = 400 \times 120 = 48{,}000$ weights. The next is $120 \times 84 \approx 10{,}000$ more. In classical convolutional networks, after downsampling you end with a couple of fully connected layers, often called the classification head. This head holds the vast majority of weights: out of roughly 60,000 total, about 57,000–58,000 lie in the head and only about 2,500 in the convolutional layers.

**[1:12:39]** The convolutional layers do the feature extraction. During backpropagation, because a weight is shared across all tiles, it appears many times in the computational graph. For a $3 \times 3$ convolution, a weight $w_i$ gets multiplied by many different pixels as the window slides, and its gradient is the sum over all those occurrences. This redundancy makes ReLU activations a good fit: sparsity is fine when you have abundant signal. Zeroing some activations does not hurt training; the weight still receives meaningful updates from other tiles.

**[1:16:03]** To summarize: we discussed how convolutional layers slide over the input, how the receptive field grows too slowly and why pooling is needed, that convolutions apply to any domain that can be tiled, and we walked through a concrete example.

**[1:16:58]** Before closing, a practical trick: padding. Applying a $3 \times 3$ convolution means not every pixel can be the center of a window, so the spatial dimensions shrink from $W \times H$ to $W-K \times H-K$ (for a $K \times K$ kernel). To preserve the tensor shape—to stack many convolutional layers without changing dimensions—you can pad the input with zeros around the border. For any pixel near the edge, you imagine zeros outside the image so a $3 \times 3$ window can be centered. This zero padding lets you keep the same spatial size after convolution.

**[1:20:27]** There are two common ways to do padding: zero padding or mirror padding. Zero padding can produce a border of implausible features because the tile picks up many zeros at the edges. Mirror padding instead copies the image's edge pixels outward as a mirror, avoiding this artifact. The whole point of padding is to fit more tiles so that any input pixel can be the center of a tile.

**[1:21:30]** If you tile with every possible tile, you will not reduce the geometry fast enough and would need pooling layers to artificially reduce it. An alternative is to not take every pixel as the tile center: for example, take every second pixel. With a 3×3 window, this yields four times fewer tiles—you halve the number of tiles across width and height. The output tensor then has size roughly $W/2$ by $H/2$, exactly the geometric reduction we often want. Despite the reduction, every pixel is still covered, just not as many times. Previously a pixel might belong to many windows; now the maximum might be four, but no information is lost, and the network can still process everything. This method is called a stride. A stride of 2 means you skip every other pixel, while stride 1 tiles every possible pixel.

**[1:24:50]** To summarize: when defining a convolutional layer, you specify the tensor shape ($d \times w \times h \times d'$), where $d$ is the input depth and $d'$ the output depth. You also define the padding (e.g., with zeros) and the stride. Formally, the stride is two-dimensional—you could take every second pixel along the x-coordinate and every third along the y-coordinate—though usually both numbers are equal. The stride defines how you tile, and the convolutional tensor size determines how many output features you get.

**[1:26:28]** A downside of using strides is that each pixel participates in fewer processing steps, which may not be desired. However, the trend in convolutional networks has been to use pooling less often because pooling can cut off computational paths and distort gradients. Modern architectures like Inception and ResNet still reduce geometry, but they often do so with a complex subnetwork that at some point applies strided convolution rather than simple pooling.
