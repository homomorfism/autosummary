**[21:06]** When did AI actually appear as a science? There are two main starting points. One is the 1943 paper on mathematical models of neural activity, which we will revisit when discussing deep learning. The other is Alan Turing’s 1950 paper introducing the Turing test.

**[21:40]** Turing was a genius who made numerous breakthroughs in computer science and essentially invented artificial intelligence. **[23:03]** The Turing test involves a judge chatting in writing with two unknown entities—a human and a computer—and trying to identify which is which. A computer program passes the Turing test only when it consistently makes it impossible for the judge to distinguish it from a human with any probability significantly different from one‑half. This formulation immediately places the test in the realm of probability theory and randomized algorithms.

**[25:19]** The most remarkable aspect of the Turing test is that, unlike many other AI benchmarks, it survived for a very long time. It was formally passed just last year, in 2025. Of course, with models like GPT‑4, no one seriously doubted they could pass in a 5–10 minute chat, but running the original test was non‑trivial because frontier models would admit they are AI if asked directly; they needed to stay in character. Small models could stay in character but would not fool anyone. Once people conducted the test properly, the models passed. This is particularly surprising because AI as a field has a long history of moving the goalposts.

**[26:56]** For instance, in the 1970s, Allen Newell wrote a position paper on the state of AI. He argued that the Turing test was not actionable then—no AI could pass it, and progress couldn’t be measured incrementally (you can’t fool 5% of judges). He called for alternative numerical tests that would reflect something inherently important about intelligence.

**[28:21]** If a machine passes the Turing test, it indicates progress toward AGI. Turing’s example was chess: he argued to Page that chess is a good candidate because it is numerical, measurable via Elo rating, and easy to program as a finite game with fixed rules. Yet chess is also deeply human—you must understand attack and defense, and perhaps model the opponent’s intentions. Thus it seemed a plausible middle ground. However, in 1997 Deep Blue beat Kasparov, and after 2001, when Kramnik narrowly missed a win, humans had no chance. By 2015, even centaur chess (human and computer collaborating) was surpassed. But AI researchers did not declare AGI; they dismissed chess as too easy because tree search alone gets near the best players. They then proposed Go, a game with a branching factor of 50–100 plausible moves, making exhaustive search impossible. Yet AlphaGo (2016) and AlphaZero soon made computers hopelessly better than humans at Go. Again, people said Go doesn’t count. This pattern of moving the goalposts continues, and people still invent reasons why LLMs don’t count.

**[32:22]** The Turing test, however, has withstood this pattern. No one in the 1980s claimed to pass it with a simple script. Why? Because the Turing test is fundamentally about freeform natural language chat. You can talk about anything, even introducing computer vision or 3D world reasoning: “What’s on your desk? What’s on your left? Walk out and describe what you see.” To maintain consistency, the other side must understand the 3D world. Natural language is complete in this sense—you can talk about any subject. This is exactly why modern LLMs are so powerful. A base LLM is trained solely to predict the next token in text. But to predict the next token well, it must learn both language structure and world knowledge. If the text says “theorem … proof,” predicting the next token may require proving the theorem. If it says “I visited Paris and went to the museum,” the model must know which museums are in Paris. Natural language thus encodes vast information about virtually everything. (An exception is robotics, where we lack massive text corpora describing joint angles for picking up a cup.) For this reason, LLMs keep surprising us with new capabilities that emerge from simple next‑token prediction.

**[43:05]** Despite the limited results, the 1950s and early 1960s were a golden age of science fiction with sky‑high expectations, expecting androids to soon walk around and converse. **[44:43]** Disillusionment in the 1960s came largely from the failed machine‑translation project. **[46:06]** The only active AI work during this period was on expert systems, which perform probabilistic reasoning on narrow domain knowledge. The prime example is MYCIN, a 1970s medical‑diagnosis system based on blood tests. A blood test gives a table of about 50 numbers. **[52:26]** To define machine learning, a classic textbook definition from the 1990s says: a computer program learns from experience $E$ with respect to a class of tasks $T$ and a performance measure $P$ if its performance on tasks in $T$, as measured by $P$, improves with experience $E$. This sounds trivial, but the key point is that we need a performance measure—some number that goes up or down.

**[53:32]** If we don't, we will never know whether we learned something. In a way, half of deep learning is about inventing loss functions—inventing the objectives. What is the number we want to minimize? How do we define the error so that minimizing it yields what we want? Sometimes it's straightforward, sometimes non-trivial; it's part of the science.

**[54:15]** You have all seen the main taxonomy of machine learning problems: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning. Supervised learning is what we intuitively think of as machine learning: building a classifier that distinguishes cats from dogs. You get a dataset of pictures, label each as cat or dog, and train a classifier to tell them apart.

**[56:19]** In supervised learning, the dataset consists of pairs $(X, Y)$. $X$ is the input, $Y$ the desired output. The model takes $X$ and produces some function $f(X)$ meant to match $Y$. For binary classification, $f(X)$ could be a number from 0 to 1 representing the probability of a cat given $X$, i.e., $p(\text{cat} \mid X)$. Mathematically, supervised learning aims to learn the conditional distribution $p(y \mid x)$; the model always assumes the input is given and cannot generate new images. In computer vision, you receive a high-dimensional input (an image or video) and break it down into answers—classification, detection, segmentation. The input is complicated, the output relatively simple.

**[59:07]** When collecting a supervised dataset, you need correct answers. Labeling in computer vision is often complicated and expensive. Classification labeling is manageable, but for tasks like segmentation, proper labeling is extremely costly, so we often work with relatively small datasets.

**[59:53]** That's only the first part. There is also unsupervised learning, where there is no $Y$, no correct answer. The dataset is just a collection of unlabeled images. What can you do? You can still pursue a goal that is very useful for downstream tasks like classification. Typically, unsupervised learning performs dimensionality reduction: compressing $X$ from its original size to a much smaller vector. Images are large—a one-megapixel photo (roughly a million pixels) has three numbers per pixel, about 3 million numbers. The space of possible images is enormous; nothing works in that dimension. The set of real images is an extremely complicated manifold in this 3-million-dimensional space. We have no idea what distinguishes real photos from random noise, except that it is an open set (tiny per-pixel changes still yield a real photo). Unsupervised learning tries to map this manifold down to a more manageable dimension, say 500, where the manifold might become more comprehensible. This is feature extraction or representation learning: mapping a large object like an image to a smaller vector without losing important information. You cannot simply take the first 100 pixels; the mapping must preserve the ability to answer interesting questions. In this compressed space, cats and dogs might become linearly separable—a hyperplane can distinguish them, whereas in the original space they are entangled. So the aim is to compress down, make the data manageable, and hope that in this new space relatively simple models can achieve what you want.

**[1:08:16]** Compression into a low-dimensional representation is never the end goal; we want features for some ultimate downstream purpose, but in unsupervised learning that purpose is not fixed. We just want to obtain good features. In deep learning, a common answer to this training‑signal problem is the autoencoder.

**[1:10:00]** We have no dataset of correct $z$ vectors, so we need a loss function. An autoencoder consists of an encoder (a neural network that compresses the input $x$ to $z$) and a decoder (another network that reconstructs $\hat{x}$ from $z$). A meaningful compression should allow faithful decompression, so the loss can be defined simply as the squared deviation between $\hat{x}$ and $x$ in pixel space, $(\hat{x} - x)^2$. The model learns to encode in a way that preserves the information needed for reconstruction.

**[1:12:46]** Unsupervised learning is fundamentally about making sense of the input data by finding structure, which often means projecting into a lower-dimensional space where patterns become clearer. The classical example is clustering, which can be viewed as an extreme form of dimensionality reduction. Clustering compresses each point to a discrete cluster label—a zero‑dimensional representation—and then attempts to reconstruct the points as faithfully as possible, e.g., by replacing each point with its cluster center. This compress‑then‑reconstruct philosophy is very natural in unsupervised learning.

**[1:15:36]** Whereas supervised learning models $p(y \mid x)$, unsupervised learning models $p(x)$ itself. Learning $p(x)$ frequently means learning a generative model that can produce new samples. In the autoencoder setting, this corresponds to feeding the decoder random noise $z$ and generating outputs that resemble the training distribution, such as new cat pictures (this generative viewpoint, while insightful, is beyond our class scope).

**[1:17:06]** Reinforcement learning is a third paradigm that we will not cover. Instead of a fixed dataset, an agent interacts with an environment and learns from the experience of rewards. For example, a chess‑playing agent learns by playing games and receiving a win/loss signal, eventually discovering a policy that maximizes cumulative reward. Reinforcement learning employs distinct techniques and is outside the scope of this course.

**[1:45:08]** In the second part today, I want to talk about what learning actually means. I recommend understanding it through the Bayesian, probabilistic perspective. For those who have had probability theory classes, you might be unhappy to hear that machine learning is pure probability theory, because such classes often involve difficult measure theory—sigma algebras, Borel sets, probability as a measure, abstract theorems. We won’t need any of that. What we need is the intuition that remains after you forget all the theorems: the basic intuition of what probability is about.

**[1:47:16]** Probability is about random variables. There are discrete random variables, such as coin tosses or die rolls, where the probabilities of outcomes sum to one. And there are continuous random variables, characterized by a density function whose integral over the probability space must equal one. In machine learning, we only deal with standard, easy probabilities that have densities. The probability spaces will only be the real line $\mathbb{R}$ to some power, Euclidean space—no exotic spaces.

**[1:49:41]** If you have two random variables, they can happen together, giving a joint probability $p(x,y)$. The marginal probability $p(x)$ is obtained by summing (or integrating) over $y$. From the joint probability, we get the conditional probability $p(x \mid y)$, which is the most important notion for machine learning. Formally, it is defined by decomposing the joint: $p(x,y) = p(x \mid y) \, p(y)$, so $p(x \mid y) = p(x,y) / p(y)$. You can also decompose the joint in the opposite order: $p(x,y) = p(y \mid x) \, p(x)$. Equating these gives Bayes’ rule: $p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid y) \, p(y)}{p(x)}$. Although this follows trivially from the definition, it philosophically underlies how real-world reasoning works—balancing probabilities and weighing evidence, because we seldom know facts for sure.

**[1:55:42]** There is also the important notion of independence: two random variables are independent if $p(x,y) = p(x)\, p(y)$. In that case, the conditional is trivial: $p(x \mid y) = p(x)$, meaning $x$ does not depend on $y$, and they can be sampled separately.

**[1:57:11]** Let’s get back to Bayes’ formula. I will restate it in a slightly different notation; it is the main formula of machine learning.

**[1:57:11]** The formula is $p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(D)}$, just with changed notation. $D$ is the dataset, and $\theta$ represents the model parameters we want to learn — for a neural net, the weights, but not as a single value, rather as a whole distribution. For now, $\theta$ can be the weights of a linear regression (our first serious example), the weights of a neural network, or simply the probability of a coin we are trying to learn.

**[1:58:58]** The central idea of machine learning is to learn the model parameters from data, i.e., to understand what parameters governed a stochastic process that produced the observed outcomes. Bayes’ theorem is the main tool for this.

**[1:59:48]** On the left, $p(\theta \mid D)$ is the posterior distribution — “after the fact,” after evidence has been included. It represents how our understanding of $\theta$ has changed given the data. The intermediate goal in learning is to infer what we can about $\theta$ from $D$; the final goal is prediction, but reaching that requires the posterior. Usually there is no way to get this distribution directly except through Bayes’ rule.

**[2:00:46]** The rule tells us the posterior is (up to a constant) the product of two things. $p(D \mid \theta)$ is the likelihood: the probability (or density) of the observed dataset given the parameters, which follows directly from the model assumptions — it is the easy part. The other factor is $p(\theta)$, the prior distribution, which encodes what we thought about $\theta$ before seeing any data. The denominator $p(D)$, often called the evidence, does not involve $\theta$ and is usually inaccessible, acting only as a normalising constant. In machine learning we therefore write $p(\theta \mid D) \propto p(D \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)$; when maximizing with respect to $\theta$, the constant can be ignored.

**[2:05:13]** As a first illustration, consider learning a coin. A coin is a stochastic process (a random variable) yielding heads or tails. It has exactly one parameter: $\theta$, the probability of heads; the probability of tails is $1-\theta$. The data $D$ is a sequence of outcomes from tossing the coin. Defining the likelihood requires the assumption of conditional independence: given $\theta$, different tosses are independent. Thus $p(D \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(d_i \mid \theta)$, where $p(d_i \mid \theta)$ is $\theta$ if $d_i$ is heads and $1-\theta$ if tails. For example, with $D = \{\text{H}, \text{T}, \text{H}, \text{H}, \text{H}, \text{T}, \text{T}\}$, the likelihood is $\theta^4 (1-\theta)^3$.

**[2:11:48]** The likelihood for a coin is $\theta^{N}(1-\theta)^{M}$, where $N$ is the number of heads and $M$ the number of tails. In machine learning, the likelihood is essentially the model itself: defining a model means writing down the assumptions that let us produce this formula. For a coin, the assumptions are two mutually exclusive outcomes, one with probability $\theta$ and the other $1-\theta$, and the tosses are conditionally independent given $\theta$.

**[2:13:22]** With only the likelihood, the main thing we can do is try to maximize it. This leads to the maximum likelihood hypothesis: $\theta_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta P(D \mid \theta)$.

**[2:14:02]** We maximize $P(D \mid \theta) = \theta^N (1-\theta)^M$. Taking the derivative and setting it to zero:
$$\frac{d}{d\theta} [\theta^N (1-\theta)^M] = N\theta^{N-1}(1-\theta)^M - M\theta^N(1-\theta)^{M-1} = 0$$
Factoring out $\theta^{N-1}(1-\theta)^{M-1}$ leaves $N(1-\theta) - M\theta = 0$, giving roots $\theta=0$, $\theta=1$, and $\theta = \frac{N}{N+M}$.

**[2:18:39]** When both heads and tails appear in the data, $\theta=0$ and $\theta=1$ give zero likelihood, so they are minima, not maxima. The maximum likelihood estimate is therefore $\theta_{\text{ML}} = \frac{N}{N+M}$. This matches the classical frequentist definition: if you toss a coin many times, the fraction of heads converges to the true probability. For a coin, there is no catch; the estimate is correct.

**[2:20:35]** Now suppose you toss a coin for the first time and get tails. The dataset is a single tail, so the likelihood is $1-\theta$. The likelihood function itself does not restrict $\theta$ to $[0,1]$; without constraints, it would be maximized at $-\infty$. If we impose $0 \le \theta \le 1$ as a constraint, the maximum likelihood estimate is $\theta=0$. The math is correct, but the conclusion is absurd: after one tail, we would claim the coin always lands tails. That makes no sense, even if I didn’t know about coins...

**[2:23:40]** We have the intuition that coins are approximately fair, so $\theta$ should be around $\frac{1}{2}$ — not exactly, but nearby. Even without that intuition, if we know nothing about coins, after a single toss we have only two options and one experiment, so the maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta$ will be either $0$ or $1$, depending on the outcome, implying that we will always observe only that result. That is not a correct conclusion. The math is correct — it is indeed the maximum likelihood hypothesis — but what is missing? More experiments are needed, of course; to get a precise estimate of $\theta$, you need more data. Statistics classes will tell you how many.

**[2:25:04]** As I said before, in the limit there is no difference. Maximum likelihood converges to the true $\theta$ as $n$ and $m$ go to infinity, and all of this discussion becomes irrelevant. If we can toss the coin infinitely often, there is no problem. But in practice, we do not live in that limit. In machine learning, we start with a given dataset — we have as much data as we have, and usually there is no easy way to get more. When we actually do machine learning, we have to assume that the dataset is fixed. Moreover, in real life, we never quite get enough data. If I have a coin and can collect more data by tossing it 50 times, I can be reasonably sure about its fairness; confidence intervals will be small. But that is not what happens with real-world problems.

**[2:27:28]** For example, suppose you are building a language model — a model that takes some text and predicts the next token. Imagine I am OpenAI, the transformer has just been introduced, and I have built my first GPT (which is half a transformer). I train it on some text dataset and obtain a pretty good language model. Then I want to see how it scales. Compared to the first GPT, it is easy to get more data — scrape the internet, collect orders of magnitude more text. With this vastly larger dataset, I might think that Bayesian considerations no longer matter and that I can train my original GPT again, nearly in the limit. But that’s not what happens. When I collect orders of magnitude more data, I do not go back to the same small model; I go to a larger model, like GPT-2, which has many more parameters. (I forget the exact numbers, but GPT-2 had about $150$ million parameters — roughly an order of magnitude more than GPT-1.) 

**[2:30:08]** In practice, when solving a difficult task such as predicting the next token or classifying images, you never have enough data. Whenever you have enough data for your model, the model is too weak, and it is better to switch to a bigger model for which you now lack data — but it will still perform better. This may be discussed later, regarding scaling laws, but the point is: in machine learning, unless you have a very specific, simple, well-defined problem (like learning a coin toss or linear regression under a strict linear assumption), if you get significantly more data, it is often best to move to a larger model, say from linear to polynomial, since the extra data guards against overfitting. Thus we usually operate at the frontier of what is possible: the data is fixed, we can select the model, and we choose a model such that there is barely enough data to train. We are always in a situation where data is scarce.

**[2:32:35]** So one toss is indeed not enough to learn the probability of a coin toss, but if that is all we have, what can we still say? We should not conclude that the coin will always land tails. What component can take us beyond the maximum likelihood hypothesis? What allows us to say that a single data point is not enough to completely shift our beliefs about the coin? The missing piece is our beliefs. If you go back to Bayes’ rule, we have only talked about the likelihood, but what we actually want…

**[02:34:02]** The posterior distribution represents my belief about the probability of heads after observing data, such as getting tails on a single coin toss. The likelihood is how likely it was to observe tails given $\theta$. To get to the posterior, the missing piece is the prior—what you believed about $\theta$ before seeing any data.

**[02:35:14]** The prior distribution $P(\theta)$ for the coin toss is a distribution on the parameters, specifically on $\theta$. Even though a coin toss seems like a purely discrete probabilistic object, as soon as we do learning, we encounter continuous distributions. $P(\theta)$ is continuous because $\theta$ is a probability. Plotting it, the first firm belief is that $\theta$ must be between 0 and 1, so $P(\theta) = 0$ outside $[0,1]$.

**[02:36:58]** This explains why the maximum likelihood estimate is not negative infinity here—the prior constrains it. With one tail as data, $P(D \mid \theta) = 1 - \theta$, a straight line. Using Bayes' rule, $P(\theta \mid D) \propto P(\theta) P(D \mid \theta)$. With a uniform prior, which is 1 on $[0,1]$, multiplying gives a curve that is 0 outside $[0,1]$ and follows $1 - \theta$ inside. This is not yet $P(\theta \mid D)$ because it lacks the normalizing denominator. The area under this curve is $\frac{1}{2}$, so to make it a proper distribution, we multiply by 2, yielding a distribution twice as large.

**[02:41:17]** The uniform prior, however, is not realistic for coins. It represents complete ignorance: any $\theta$ in $[0,1]$ is equally likely. In reality, when taking a physical coin, you expect it to be approximately fair—$\theta$ is close to $\frac{1}{2}$, not exactly due to imperfections. So my actual prior is still zero outside $[0,1]$, but inside it has a bell-shaped curve peaking near $\frac{1}{2}$. My intuition doesn't specify the exact shape, so I cannot define it mathematically.

**[02:43:37]** Nevertheless, let's first complete the uniform prior case. While not realistic for coins, it is perfectly reasonable in many situations as a representation of no prior knowledge. For a new problem where a parameter is known to be a probability, a uniform distribution is a great starting point.

**[02:44:20]** Thus, with a uniform prior, multiplying by the likelihood $1 - \theta$ and normalizing gives $P(\theta \mid D) = 2(1 - \theta)$ for $\theta \in [0,1]$, and 0 otherwise. Now we can ask: given the posterior distribution, what is the maximum a posteriori hypothesis—the $\theta$ that maximizes the posterior?

**[2:45:39]** $P(\theta \mid D)$? In this case, with a uniform prior the only thing the prior does is rule out degenerate solutions—it says $\theta$ should be between 0 and 1. **[2:46:59]** But even for a uniform prior, something has changed because I have one more point to make in this example. In a machine learning problem like distinguishing cats from dogs, what is the end goal of learning the parameters? The final goal is not $\theta$ itself. So far we have been talking about learning something about $\theta$: $\theta_{\text{ML}}$ and $\theta_{\text{MAP}}$. If you map this to neural networks, $\theta$ is the vector of weights, and $\theta_{\text{MAP}}$ is just a checkpoint of those weights. That is an intermediate step, not the goal. I don’t care what the weights are; I want to distinguish cats from dogs. What we are supposed to do—in machine learning and generally in life—is make predictions. Philosophically, understanding something means you can predict what will happen and be right more often than chance. It means operationalizing your knowledge to predict things you didn’t know in a way that conforms to reality. Even in the simple coin toss case, where predicting heads or tails seems equivalent to learning the probability, there is a difference. The predictive distribution is $P(X \mid D)$, the probability of the next outcome given the data. It does not contain $\theta$; I don’t care about $\theta$ here, I care about solving the problem. But I cannot get this distribution without introducing $\theta$. The only way to compute it is through the joint probability and marginalization: $P(X \mid D) = \int P(X, \theta \mid D)\, d\theta = \int P(X \mid \theta, D) P(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta$. Since data points are independent given the parameters, $P(X \mid \theta, D) = P(X \mid \theta)$. So the predictive distribution is $\int P(X \mid \theta) P(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta$.

**[2:54:00]** For our coin example, even with a uniform prior, this predictive distribution differs from simply using the point estimate. $P(\text{heads} \mid D) = \int_0^1 \theta \cdot P(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta$. The posterior $P(\theta \mid D) \propto \theta^N (1-\theta)^M$ on $[0,1]$ and zero elsewhere. So $P(\text{heads} \mid D) = \frac{\int_0^1 \theta^{N+1} (1-\theta)^M d\theta}{\int_0^1 \theta^N (1-\theta)^M d\theta}$. Evaluating these integrals (which reduce to the beta function) gives $\frac{N+1}{N+M+2}$. This is Laplace’s rule of succession. The predictive distribution is the expectation over the posterior of the likelihood, integrating over all possible $\theta$ rather than using a single point estimate.

**[2:57:41]** This is the likelihood averaged over the posterior distribution. In our coin example, we average over the posterior (the purple distribution). Although its maximum is at zero (the MAP estimate), averaging over the whole distribution yields a value different from simply plugging in zero. The average is not one half nor zero, but something like one third. So you average your likelihood over all possibilities for the model parameters.

**[2:59:21]** This kind of reasoning is central to machine learning. When you define a model—for example, to distinguish cats from dogs—you define a loss function. Almost always, the loss function is the negative log likelihood you want to minimize, or even more often, the negative log posterior. The log posterior is proportional to the log likelihood plus the log prior plus a constant. Thus, in optimization, you are effectively maximizing the log likelihood plus the log prior, where the log prior acts as a regularizer. Regularization means putting a prior on the model parameters, introducing your own beliefs about the parameters.

**[3:01:34]** The formulas so far used a uniform prior, but for a real coin we expect a bell-shaped curve near one half. To use a more realistic prior, we must define $p(\theta)$. The prior on $\theta$ should be zero outside $[0,1]$, but the shape inside is not obvious. A natural candidate is a normal distribution, but the normal has tails that never reach zero. We could truncate and renormalize it: $p(\theta) = C \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(\theta - \frac12)^2\right)$ on $[0,1]$, zero elsewhere. Multiplying this by the likelihood $\theta^n (1-\theta)^m$ gives a posterior proportional to $C \exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(\theta - \frac12)^2) \theta^n (1-\theta)^m$, an exponent times a polynomial. This is inconvenient because the shapes do not match: the prior is Gaussian-like, the likelihood is polynomial, and the product does not reduce to a simple closed form. While numerical methods work for a one-dimensional parameter, this prior is not mathematically convenient.

**[3:05:58]** Since intuition does not specify whether the bell shape is normal, Student's t, or something else, we can choose the prior for convenience. The key is to select a prior whose functional form matches the likelihood, so their multiplication remains tractable. The likelihood, fixed by the data-generating process, is $\theta^n (1-\theta)^m$. The easiest function to multiply with it is one of the same form: a prior proportional to $\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}$ (normalizing constant is a Beta function). This is the Beta distribution with parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Multiplying this prior by the likelihood yields a posterior proportional to $\theta^{\alpha+n-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+m-1}$, which is again a Beta distribution, now with parameters $\alpha+n$ and $\beta+m$. The posterior has the same form as the prior—this is called a conjugate prior. Conjugacy trivializes Bayes' rule: updating the prior after seeing data reduces to simple addition of the exponents. We can move from prior to posterior for any $n$ and $m$ without new math or numerical algorithms.

**[3:09:49]** To confirm that this kind of prior can express a bell-shaped curve, if I have $\theta^{10}$ and $(1-\theta)^{10}$ with equal exponents, I get a peak at one half. The beta family of distributions is shown here. The uniform distribution is a beta with parameters $(1,1)$, i.e. $\theta^{1-1} (1-\theta)^{1-1} = 1$. As $\alpha$ and $\beta$ increase, the distributions become more pointy; the variance decreases. When $\alpha = \beta$, the maximum is at $1/2$, and in general the maximum is at $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$, just like the maximum likelihood. So the beta family includes what I want, and many other nice distributions that can appear as posteriors.

**[3:11:28]** What does actual Bayesian inference look like? Starting with a uniform prior, the likelihood for one tails is multiplied by the prior, renormalized, and gives the posterior. If the prior had parameters $(2,2)$ instead, meaning $\theta^1 (1-\theta)^1$, it is a parabola. Multiplying this prior by the same likelihood (one tails) yields a cubic curve shifted to the left with maximum at $1/3$. You can continue this: you can become more sure a priori that the coin was fair, obtaining increasingly peaked priors around $1/2$. The more convinced you are in advance, the less a single experiment matters; if you were very sure the coin is fair, one tails will barely change the posterior. But if you keep tossing and getting tails, it matters more: 10 tails shifts the posterior left, and 100 tails shifts it quite a lot. The prior strongly influences how much evidence is needed to move your beliefs.

**[3:14:25]** Another nice property of a situation where the posterior has the same form as the prior (conjugate priors) is that you can reuse the posterior as the prior for the next experiment, enabling sequential updating. Start with a uniform prior; after tossing heads once, the posterior is a straight line. After another toss (tails), the posterior becomes a parabola. For three more tosses, you do not need to restart from scratch: take that parabola as the prior, multiply by the likelihood of the next three observations, and obtain the new posterior. You can forget the previous dataset and just plug the current posterior in as the next prior.

**[3:16:06]** Formally, you start with $p(\theta)$, obtain $p(\theta \mid D)$ from a dataset, and then for more data $D'$, you want $p(\theta \mid D, D')$. With a conjugate prior, you can simply treat $p(\theta \mid D)$ as the new prior and apply Bayes’ rule again to get $p(\theta \mid D, D')$; it is the same rule with $D$ conditioning on the right throughout. Without conjugacy, it would be much harder, and you would prefer to compute from scratch with $D \cup D'$.

**[3:17:14]** Finally, here is a sequence of coin tosses and the evolving probability of heads. The blue line is the maximum likelihood hypothesis; the orange line is Laplace’s rule, i.e. the predictive distribution $\frac{n+1}{n+m+2}$. In the beginning, Laplace’s rule is less extreme: after one toss, maximum likelihood is either $0$ or $1$, while Laplace gives $1/3$ or $2/3$, much closer to $1/2$. It moderates the extremes of maximum likelihood, but after about 50 tosses they become indistinguishable; both converge to the true probability.

The most difficult problem is obtaining the predictive distribution of the next data point, regardless of the specific $\theta$, which requires averaging over $\theta$.
