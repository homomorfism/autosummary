**[00:16]** The main advantage of convolutional neural networks is weight reuse: the same weights are applied over the entire input and across all intermediate feature tensors (outputs of intermediate layers), enabling parallel processing and drastically reducing the number of parameters.

**[02:30]** In classical computer vision, before neural networks, convolutions were used as handcrafted filters—small matrices applied in parallel over an image for tasks like sharpening, blurring, adding contrast, embossing, and edge detection. For example, Gabor filters perform directed edge detection by computing discrete derivatives (finite differences) in different directions: one filter activates on large vertical differences between adjacent pixels, another on horizontal changes. Applying pooling as an image filter: max pooling would clamp to maximum brightness, producing a strange result, while average pooling appears smoother—though this is not its purpose.

**[05:06]** In real networks like ResNet-18, the first layer’s convolutional filters can be examined. Some detect diagonal or vertical lines, others identify colors or color gradients. With an input image, one filter might fire where green appears (grass/trees), another for horizontal edges. Deeper in the network, the direct pixel-to-feature correspondence vanishes—each feature has a receptive field, but pinpointing the exact contributing pixels requires additional math. Nevertheless, you can observe what upper-layer features respond to. In ResNet-18, the second layer fires for high-frequency content (stripes, edges) across the image. Deeper layers activate for more semantic, salient objects: a filter might fire for eyes and ear tips, and even deeper ones respond to entire heads. Visualizations become increasingly blurry and less geometric, but deep features can be mapped back to the original image to approximate their triggers.

**[12:18]** The most important convolutional architecture in history was AlexNet—the first truly effective convolutional network. Technically, AlexNet’s two key ideas are: first, it is split into two parallel streams, making it the first industrial-scale instance of model parallelization—a very important concept in deep learning.

**[14:24]** To train with multiple GPUs, there are two main approaches. Data parallelization splits a mini-batch across GPUs, processes them in parallel, collects the results, and performs a single update based on all weight updates. The network implementation does not change; this simply increases the effective mini-batch size by spreading the batch.

**[15:54]** Model parallelization assumes the model does not fit on one GPU. You split the model across GPUs. AlexNet (implemented by Alex Krizhevsky in 2011, published 2012) did exactly this by hand—a major engineering feat at the time. Today, frameworks like PyTorch allow you to assign specific weight matrices to specific GPUs and automatically handle communication. When designing such a network, you want to minimize cross-GPU communication because it is slower than on-chip. For instance, a design might have only two points where the GPUs exchange information.

**[17:31]** AlexNet was the breakthrough moment. Afterwards, the field exploded with convolutional networks, and many improvements followed from relatively simple ideas. The first, already discussed with Alex (not Krizhevsky, but Alex Davidov), is VGG. AlexNet still used $11 \times 11$ convolutions in the first layer, but now we rarely see them. VGG showed that large convolutions can be represented as compositions of small ones, typically stacks of $3 \times 3$ (sometimes $5 \times 5$). This is like a convolution inside a convolution—it further reduces the number of weights (as you computed) and acts as additional structural regularization, since the network processes different parts of the input in the same way. It usually works better.

**[19:12]** The second idea is Inception (GoogLeNet) and the broader concept of Network in Network. Earlier, we discussed processing image tiles with small neural networks that consisted of a single weight matrix applied to the concatenated input. The inception trick replaces that simple convolution with a more complex mini-network, but still applies it identically across tiles. Notably, these inception units often feature multiple parallel processing branches, which is no coincidence and we will discuss further today.

**[21:46]** The third and most important idea is residual connections, giving rise to ResNet. If a layer’s output $f(x)$ saturates and its gradients vanish, without a skip connection all layers below would stop receiving gradients (since gradients flow through derivatives that go to zero). The residual connection adds the input: $y = f(x) + x$. When $f(x)$ stops learning, gradients still flow through the identity path. Kaiming He introduced ResNets and called it the “revolution of depth.” With residual connections, networks with hundreds of layers could be trained: AlexNet had 8 trainable layers, VGG had 19, GoogLeNet 22, and ResNet immediately reached 150, and you could go to 200, 500, or even 1000 layers without training issues. The only limit is diminishing returns; practical ResNets go to about 200 layers.

**[25:44]** Alex stopped around here, so let me continue. Our first new point is bottleneck layers and the split-transform-merge idea. The bottleneck idea, like VGG, reduces weight count further. VGG replaced a large $n \times n$ convolution (with $n^2$ weights) by a sequence of small convolutions (linear in $n$ weights). Convolutions already save a huge number of weights, but we can do even better. Let’s step back and analyze a standard convolutional layer. Its input is a 3D tensor. Consider just the window that the filter processes; the operation is replicated over the full input.

**[27:52]** We have a cut-out window of depth $D$, width $W$, height $H$; a standard convolutional layer then produces a single feature vector of size $D'$, with a weight tensor of dimensions $D \times W \times H \times D'$. This is far better than a fully connected layer, but the parameter count can still grow quickly. For example, if $D = 256$, $D' = 256$, and even with VGG‑style small filters $W = H = 3$, the tensor is $256 \times 3 \times 3 \times 256$ — about 590K parameters.

**[29:56]** How can we reduce this? The bottleneck idea is straightforward. Instead of a full convolution with $D \times D' \times 9$ weights, we first compress the features with a $1 \times 1$ convolution, then do the spatial convolution, then expand back. Suppose we take an input of size $D \times W \times H$ and pass it through a $1 \times 1$ convolution that reduces the depth to $D''$ (e.g., 32). Then we apply the spatial ($W \times H$) convolution, and finally another $1 \times 1$ convolution to restore the depth to $D'$ (256). The parameter count becomes $D \cdot D'' + D'' \cdot W \cdot H \cdot D'' + D'' \cdot D'$, i.e., $256 \times 32 + 32 \times 32 \times 9 + 32 \times 256 = 256 \times 100 = 25.6\text{K}$, compared to the original $256 \times 256 \times 9 \approx 590\text{K}$ — about a 25‑fold saving.

**[34:42]** But something should feel off: we took a long 256‑dimensional feature vector, compressed it to 32 numbers, did interesting stuff there, and then decompressed back to 256. That decompression cannot restore the lost information. In a single branch, this makes no sense — we could simply let the previous layer produce 32 features instead and save even more weights. The bottleneck idea alone does not work, and it never appears that way.

**[37:56]** It becomes useful when we use multiple parallel branches. Instead of one processing pipeline, we create several copies of this bottleneck block, each with its own independently learned weights, and then concatenate their outputs. With $D = 256$ and a bottleneck to $D'' = 32$, we need eight such branches to reconstruct a 256‑dimensional output. The parameter cost per branch is still $25.6\text{K}$, so $8 \times 25.6\text{K} \approx 205\text{K}$ total, compared to the original $590\text{K}$ — about a 3–4‑fold saving. Geometrically, we are not taking one low‑dimensional projection but multiple different projections — different viewpoints — of the same high‑dimensional feature space, each capturing a different aspect of the data.

**[40:59]** Not only can you train the network to avoid losing important information, but it can even outperform processing the entire high-dimensional object at once. In fact, doing many random projections of a high-dimensional object is often a good idea, and here the projections are not random but learned to be useful, which is even better. This approach is called split‑transform‑merge: first split the input, then transform each part, then merge the results. It is a standard idea in convolutional architectures.

**[42:20]** For instance, in ResNeXt (now outdated, about 10 years old), you split the 256 channels into 32 paths of width 4, implementing multiple extreme projections that work better together.

**[42:41]** The Inception and Inception‑ResNet families were a highly important line of networks and for a long time served as the best backbones for all sorts of computer vision tasks. When tackling tasks like object detection or segmentation, there is rarely enough labeled data to train a network from scratch. The standard practice is to take a backbone pretrained on ImageNet for classification, cut off its classification head, and use the rest of the network as a feature extractor, possibly fine‑tuning it slightly. The Inception‑ResNet family was one of the most influential backbones around 2016.

**[44:25]** The main idea was to design progressively better basic blocks—small neural networks that process the inputs. Another important advance was replacing pooling layers with learnable reduction blocks. When you need to reduce spatial dimension, e.g., from 256×256 to 128×128, max pooling can be used, but it is better to let a trainable layer do more interesting work. This is achieved by composing layers with strided convolutions so that the output becomes smaller. These reduction blocks, sometimes containing pooling internally, again follow the split‑transform‑merge pattern with several parallel processing lines whose results are merged. Adding residual connections yields Inception‑ResNet. Inception‑ResNet V2 was a very influential and widely used backbone.

**[46:52]** Beyond this, there were ideas to further reduce network size and the number of layers. Two objectives must be balanced: memory (number of weights) and latency (number of layers). A smaller network reduces memory, but latency depends more on the number of layers because operations within a layer are parallelized on the GPU; the number of sequential steps matters most, though the size of intermediate representations also plays a role. Latency is critical for real‑time applications like surveillance tracking, which must run at least 30–60 fps. Networks such as SqueezeNet and MobileNet targeted these efficiency goals, with MobileNet designed specifically to bring computer vision to mobile devices.

**[50:20]** The line of networks that topped this architectural progress is EfficientNet. Its design was found automatically via large‑scale neural architecture search. The search considered how to scale a convolutional network: there are three main knobs—depth (number of layers), width (number of channels), and resolution (input size). When scaling, if depth grows exponentially with exponent α, width with β, and resolution with γ, the total number of weights scales as their product. They ran an evolutionary search to find the optimal balance of α, β, and γ. The resulting EfficientNet family defined the state of the art across the field.

**[54:45]** This means your best networks lie in the top left corner: you want a small network that works very well. There is a trade-off; large networks will work better than small ones. The EfficientNet family succeeded in defining this frontier everywhere. Since then, I think there has been no revolution in convolutional networks — to a large extent everyone switched to transformers. **[58:19]** With that, we are done with convolutional networks. You will encounter more of them in practical sessions. Now we move to the next big topic: recurrent neural networks. Until transformers appeared, there were two main architectural strands in deep learning — convolutional networks and recurrent networks. Recurrent networks were specifically designed for sequences.

**[59:40]** Often your input takes the form of a sequence. An image is not really a sequence — it is a two-dimensional structure, so CNNs are the natural choice. But many input types are inherently sequences. The default example is a time series, like financial data. Sound is a sequence of amplitude ticks; in classical machine learning you would not treat sound as a raw amplitude sequence because it would be too long. Instead you cut it into packets of 20–30 milliseconds, extract features, and then you have a sequence of feature vectors. Text is definitely a sequence of tokens. It may be words or tokens, but it has a one-dimensional structure.

**[1:02:06]** When working with sequences, there are several ways to frame the problem. The simplest is a sequence input with a single output, such as text classification — you have a news article and you want to say whether it is in sports, politics, or science. The reverse is a single input that must be unrolled into an output sequence — sequence generation. For example, image captioning: the input is an image (not a sequence), and you convert it into a text sequence describing what is in the image. The most interesting problems are sequence-to-sequence ones, where both input and output are sequences, like machine translation.

**[1:04:16]** You can also have sequence labeling problems, where both input and output are sequences but the output is aligned to the input. In NLP this includes part-of-speech tagging — for every word in the sequence you assign a label. Another example is adding subtitles to a movie: the video is a sequence of frames (each frame an image), and for every frame you want the specific text that should appear. Object tracking is a similar case from computer vision: you follow an object through a video, performing segmentation on the sequence of frames so that the same object is tracked as it moves.

**[1:06:36]** Convolutional networks are not well suited for dealing with sequences. You can apply a sliding window along the input sequence, and for a time convolutional approaches to NLP rivaled recurrent ones — before transformers changed everything. But the crucial missing capability is the ability to recall inputs from long ago; we need to understand the whole sentence and retain information across time.

**[1:08:00]** When you read a paragraph, you need to remember what was happening in the sequence a long time ago; otherwise the end won't make sense. How can we achieve this with a neural architecture? Recurrent neural networks do it like this.

**[1:08:21]** Let's say we have an input $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_t$. We process each input in the sequence separately with a small neural network called a recurrent unit. This unit has two inputs and one or two outputs: it takes $x$ and also a hidden state $h$. Its main job is to process this new input and update $h$ so that now it captures the sequence up to and including $x$, assuming $h$ captured the previous part.

**[1:09:30]** Formally, the recurrent unit is a function $f(x, h)$ that outputs an updated hidden state $h'$ and maybe an output. The hidden state is a vector of numbers that travels along the network and is updated every time we read another input. We go through the sequence, and this hidden state hopefully combines all the relevant information so that we can just look at it and say what the sequence is about. We initialize with some $h_0$; then the outputs are $h_1, h_2, \dots, h_t, h_{t+1}$, etc.

**[1:11:23]** In classical recurrent networks, the recurrent cell is a simple linear layer: you multiply $x$ by a matrix $A$, $h$ by a matrix $B$, add them, and pass the result through an activation function $g$, such as $\tanh$ or ELU: $h' = g(Ax + Bh)$. $A$ and $B$ are weight matrices.

**[1:12:46]** Recurrent networks have been around since the second half of the 1980s, but people didn't have enough compute to train them properly. We will discuss the training difficulties shortly.

**[1:13:36]** An important general remark: a unidirectional RNN reads the input left to right. For whole-sequence classification using the last hidden state, that may suffice. But for sequence labeling — for example, part-of-speech tagging, where we need to output $y_t$ at each step — a left-to-right RNN only sees words before $x_t$, which is unnatural. Therefore, we often use bidirectional RNNs. We have two separate recurrent networks: one reads the sequence left-to-right, the other reads the same input right-to-left. At position $x_t$, the left-to-right hidden state captures left context, and the right-to-left hidden state captures right context. We can merge them with a classification head to output $y_t$, accounting for both past and future context.

**[1:17:55]** Unless the problem itself prohibits it, there is no reason not to use a bidirectional RNN. For example, in language modeling you predict the next word given only the left context, so a unidirectional model is required. In real-time object tracking, you have a video stream and can only look a few frames ahead, so you need a one-sided network. But in many problems (e.g., sequence-to-sequence tasks), you are allowed to read the entire input before making a decision, so bidirectional RNNs are standard.

**[1:19:38]** Now let's discuss the problems with recurrent networks. How does a recurrent neural network train? Consider a unidirectional RNN: $x_t, x_{t+1}$ like this and we go to $x_t$.

**[1:20:36]** Assume we only need the output at the end, for example after reading the whole sequence we classify it. We have some head, then the loss function compares the predicted answer to the correct answer, as usual. The key is that every recurrent cell works exactly the same way, with the exact same weights. In the computational graph, the weights of a cell are shared across all recurrent cells — it is the same matrix $W$, the same weight tensor participating in every cell. We cannot have separate matrices for each step because training would be impossible. Moreover, this sharing allows processing sequences of arbitrary length with the same $W$, a very useful property. But it also leads to interesting problems.

**[1:22:44]** The gradient of the loss with respect to a specific weight $w$ is a large sum because $w$ influences the loss at every time step: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_t} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{t-1}} + \dots + \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}$. These are different participations of the same weight in the computational graph, not different weights. The same weight is used many times along the sequence. On one hand this is good, like in convolutional networks where weight sharing allows sparse activations to accumulate into meaningful gradients. But on the other hand, there is a problem with this repeated influence of $W$ through the whole sequence. It is easier to see during the forward pass. Suppose for simplicity we just multiply the state $H$ by $W$ repeatedly: $H_1 = W H_0$, $H_2 = W H_1 = W^2 H_0$, and in general $H_T = W^T H_0$. This simplified picture ignores activations and inputs, but suffices. Matrices have a spectral radius $\rho$ (you can also think of the Frobenius norm), which tells you how multiplying by the matrix scales the length of a vector. Roughly, $\|H_t\| \approx \rho \|H_{t-1}\|$, so $\|H_t\| \approx \rho^t \|H_0\|$. If $\rho > 1$, the activations explode exponentially with sequence length, causing numerical overflow and meaningless huge values. The same happens in the backward pass: gradient computation involves multiplication by $W^\top$, so we are again sensitive to $\rho$. Thus we can have exploding gradients when $\rho > 1$ (everything goes to infinity) or vanishing gradients when $\rho < 1$ (everything goes to zero).

**[1:29:38]** This means that as the gradient travels back, it exponentially vanishes for early sequence elements, destroying long-term memory. The influence of earlier parts vanishes completely. Exploding gradients break everything; vanishing gradients are not catastrophic but prevent the network from working as desired. Vanishing gradients will be discussed tomorrow. Exploding gradients have a very simple fix: as you go through the network, the gradient may grow uncontrollably and its length increases. You can notice this and simply clip the gradient. If the gradient norm exceeds a threshold, project it back to the unit sphere. After the next step it will be too long again, and you clip again. That is the only solution needed.
